## Q1. Downstroke vs upstroke: what the shoulder, elbow and wrist do, how much the wing folds, and how this changes with speed and species

### Takeaway
The downstroke is the power stroke. The wing is fully extended and swept down (and forward relative to the body in level flight), with the humerus depressing through roughly 100 degrees in steep, slow flight. The upstroke is a recovery stroke. The shoulder elevates and supinates the humerus while the elbow and wrist fold the wing. In XROMM data from chukars, the elbow flexes through about 90 degrees (from about 127 to 35 degrees) and the wrist through about 90 degrees (from about 148 to 58 degrees). How much the wing folds depends on speed and wing shape. Birds with pointed wings use a "tip-reversal" upstroke at low speed and a "swept-wing" upstroke at higher speed. Birds with rounded wings, such as magpies, use a flexed, "feathered" upstroke at every speed. Hummingbirds barely fold at all.

### Cited Findings

**Joint-level skeletal kinematics: chukar (*Alectoris chukar*), XROMM, ascending flight at about 2 m/s [FF-lab] (2 birds, 4 trials)**
- The glenohumeral (shoulder) joint drives most of the wing's movement. The more distal joints mainly change wing shape. [Baier, Gatesy & Dial 2013, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- Shoulder elevation/depression has a range of 101.6 ± 5.4 degrees, from a maximum of +101.1 to a minimum of about −0.4 degrees. Peak depression comes at 92.0 ± 9.3% of the downstroke, and peak elevation at about 67.6 ± 10.5% of the upstroke. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- Shoulder protraction/retraction has a range of 45.5 ± 5.9 degrees (−6.8 to −52.3 degrees). In the body frame, the humerus mainly retracts during the downstroke and protracts during the upstroke. In ascending flight, retraction ends at the downstroke-to-upstroke transition. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- Shoulder pronation/supination (long-axis rotation) has a range of 35.8 ± 15.1 degrees (−48.8 to −84.6 degrees). Supination begins about midway through the downstroke, at 29.4 ± 3.8% of the wingstroke. Ascending flight also shows a second pronation phase during the upstroke. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- Elbow flexion/extension has a range of 91.3 ± 5.2 degrees, from a maximum extension of 126.7 to a minimum of 35.4 degrees. Elbow abduction/adduction has a range of 47.0 ± 9.4 degrees. Peak elbow extension comes at 46 ± 15% of the downstroke and peak flexion at 45 ± 9% of the upstroke. Early in the downstroke the ulna re-extends from the previous cycle, then flexes for the rest of the downstroke. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- Wrist flexion/extension has a range of 90.0 ± 17.8 degrees (147.9 to 57.9 degrees). Wrist abduction/adduction has a range of 79.4 ± 5.4 degrees, with minimum adduction at −69.9 degrees. In ascending flight the wrist stays extended into the upstroke, until about 40% of the upstroke, after the elbow has already fully flexed. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- Proximal-to-distal sequencing: at the down-to-up turnaround, the elbow reverses about 4.0 ± 2.1% of the cycle before the fingertip, and the wrist about 2.0 ± 2.2% before it. The humerus starts moving upward first, then the wrist, then the fingertip. At the up-to-down turnaround the joints and the tip are nearly synchronous (+0.4 to +0.5% of the cycle). [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- Contrast with wing-assisted incline running (WAIR): humeral depression is about 50% smaller (elevation/depression range 47.6 degrees). Elbow and wrist excursions are also smaller (61 and 48 degrees), and wrist flexion starts earlier in the downstroke. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- The furcula (wishbone) spreads by 5.0 mm on a 23.0 mm resting width, to about 129% of resting. It is widest (29.6 mm) at the upstroke-to-downstroke transition and narrowest (24.6 mm) at 80% of the downstroke. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)

**Pigeon (*Columba livia*) joint coupling**
- In free-flying pigeons, a joint model treats the shoulder as 3 degrees of freedom (DOF), the elbow as 1 and the wrist as 2. Shoulder roll and wrist roll are coupled during the upstroke. The yaw angles of the shoulder, elbow and wrist are coupled in both strokes. This yaw coupling, out of the stroke plane, produces wing sweep and folding. [Shen et al. 2024, Biomimetics](https://mdpi.com/2313-7673/9/9/555) (abstract/snippet only; full-text joint-angle tables could not be retrieved) [FF-lab]
- Elbow and wrist motion are mechanically coupled, but not by the classical planar "drawing parallels" mechanism proposed in 1839. Skeletal paths are strongly three-dimensional. A six-bar linkage, with a parallel radius and ulna plus a **sliding ulnare** wrist bone, reproduces the measured motion. [Stowers, Matloff & Lentink 2017, J R Soc Interface](https://www.peeref.com/works/4113142); [supplementary data](https://rs.figshare.com/collections/Supplementary_material_from_How_pigeons_couple_three-dimensional_elbow_and_wrist_motion_to_morph_their_wings_/3832504) [ex vivo + micro-CT]
- During wing extension the ulnare slides mainly along the ulna, while the radiale stays relatively stationary. The second digit can move independently by up to about 30 degrees, for fine control of the wingtip. [Avian biology course notes, avesbiology.com (summarizing Stowers et al. 2017)](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary source)

**What each stroke does, in functional terms**
- Downstroke: the wing moves down and forward, with the leading edge of the distal wing lower than its trailing edge. This tilts the resultant force forward, producing thrust. The proximal wing moves little and provides most of the lift. The distal wing sweeps a wide arc and produces thrust. [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
- Upstroke: the wing is partly folded at the wrist and elbow and drawn toward the body to reduce drag. The tips of the primaries separate. [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)

**Speed and species dependence of upstroke style (wind tunnel)**
- Tobalske, Hedrick & Biewener (2003) filmed budgerigar, cockatiel, ringed turtle dove and black-billed magpie at 250 Hz, from 1 m/s up to maximum speed (14–17 m/s) in 2 m/s steps. The three pointed, high-aspect-ratio species used a **wing-tip reversal upstroke**, with the distal wing supinated at mid-upstroke, at equivalent airspeeds of **≤ 7–9 m/s**. Above that they switched to a **swept-wing upstroke without supination**. The magpie (rounded, low-aspect-ratio wings) used a **flexed-wing, feathered upstroke at all steady speeds**. In the pointed-wing species, wingspan exceeded wrist span at mid-upstroke at every speed. In the magpie, wrist span was greater than or equal to wingspan, meaning the hand was folded in. Kinematics "vary gradually as a function of flight speed". [Tobalske, Hedrick & Biewener 2003, J Avian Biol 34:177](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/wing-kinematics-of-avian-flight-across-speeds/) [WT]
- Cockatiels and ringed turtle-doves were also analyzed in 3D at 1–15 m/s, to estimate circulation and gait changes. [Hedrick, Tobalske & Biewener 2002](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/estimates-circulation-and-gait-change-based-three-dimensional-kinematic) (title/snippet only) [WT]
- Rufous hummingbird (3 g, N = 5, 0–12 m/s): the wings are "kinematically rigid", flexing only slightly at the wrist during the upstroke. The average span ratio is 93%. [Tobalske et al. 2007, J Exp Biol 210:2368](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/three-dimensional-kinematics-of-hummingbird-flight/) [WT]
- House martin (*Delichon urbica*) at 8–10 m/s: there is a characteristic pause in mid-upstroke, with the wingtips held close to the body (a strongly flexed upstroke). The span ratio is about 0.31–0.41. [Rosén, Spedding & Hedenström 2007, J R Soc Interface](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391) [WT]

**Why the wing folds on the upstroke (energetics)**
- In a pigeon optimal-control model (0.4 kg bird, wing mass 0.0258 kg), predicted power at 6 m/s is over 34 W, but only 8.7 W if wing mass is ignored. Wing inertia therefore dominates the cost at slow speed. A flexed upstroke used up to 25% less power than a fully extended upstroke in one optimal mode. In another mode, a fully extended upstroke cut power by up to 55% relative to a flexed (E = 0.25) case. Whether folding pays off depends on speed and flight mode. [Parslew 2015, J R Soc Interface](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085) [model]
- Most birds in forward flight reduce span on the upstroke by flexing the wing. This avoids producing negative thrust as large as the positive thrust of the downstroke. [Search-result summary of Tobalske 2007 J Exp Biol 210:3135](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/210/18/3135/1258667/3135.pdf) (snippet only; full text 403)

### Inferences
- **Keyframe recipe for one cycle (phase 0 = top of the stroke, start of the downstroke)**, built from the chukar XROMM timings and the pointed-wing wind-tunnel styles. Ascending-flight chukar values are the best quantitative joint data found, so treat them as an upper bound on excursions for level cruising.
  1. *Start of downstroke (0%)*: humerus fully elevated (about +100 degrees in chukar). Elbow mid-way through re-extending, wrist extended, primaries closed into a continuous surface. Furcula at its widest.
  2. *Early to mid-downstroke (0–25% of cycle)*: humerus depresses rapidly and pronates (leading edge down). Elbow reaches peak extension near mid-downstroke, giving maximum span. Peak pectoralis force falls here (see Q6).
  3. *Late downstroke (25–50%)*: humerus keeps depressing toward about horizontal or slightly below. Supination begins about mid-downstroke. The elbow starts flexing. The wrist is still extended.
  4. *Down-to-up transition*: the humerus reverses first, then the elbow (about 4% of the cycle before the tip), then the wrist (about 2% before), then the tip. This proximal-to-distal lag is what makes the wingtip "whip" at the bottom.
  5. *Early upstroke*: humerus elevates and supinates while the elbow flexes hard. For the tip-reversal style the hand supinates and flicks backward. For the flexed style the wrist also folds and pulls the hand toward the body.
  6. *Mid-upstroke (about 45% into the upstroke)*: elbow at peak flexion (about 35 degrees included angle in chukar). The wrist starts flexing at about 40% into the upstroke, so maximum span reduction falls in mid to late upstroke. Primaries are separated.
  7. *Late upstroke*: elbow and wrist re-extend and the hand pronates back, ready for the next downstroke.
- **Folding magnitude by style** (inferred from the span ratios in Q5):
  - Hummingbird: about 7% span loss.
  - Swept-wing upstroke in fast pointed-wing birds: moderate folding. The hand stays extended, so wingspan exceeds wrist span.
  - Flexed upstroke in house martins and magpies: about 60–70% span loss.
- **The direction of the downstroke depends on the reference frame.** "Down and forward" describes the tip path relative to the air or the stroke plane in level flight. In steep, nose-up ascending flight (chukar body pitch about 53 degrees), the humerus retracts toward the tail in the body frame during the downstroke. An animator should define the stroke plane relative to the body and then pitch the body.

### Gaps
- No full-text joint-angle time series were retrieved for **level cruising flight** in a common species (for example, a pigeon at 8–12 m/s). The chukar XROMM data are for steep (about 60 degree) ascending flight at about 2 m/s. Shen et al. 2024 (pigeon) likely has these numbers, but the PDF exceeded the fetch size limit.
- Tobalske & Dial 1996 (magpie and pigeon, 4–14 m/s) and Tobalske 2007 (review) were blocked, so per-speed elbow and wrist angles from those papers are missing.
- Background not verified in this session: the supracoracoideus tendon passes through the triosseal canal and inserts on the dorsal humerus, which is why a ventral muscle can lift the wing.

## Q2. Downstroke-to-upstroke timing ratio and wingtip path shape (side and front views)

### Takeaway
The downstroke fraction of the cycle is not fixed. It ranges from about 0.32 to 0.5 in the free-flight and wind-tunnel data found. In the house martin it falls as speed rises, because downstroke duration stays roughly constant while the upstroke lengthens. Hovering hummingbirds trace a figure-8 wingtip path, which opens into an ellipse in forward flight. Flexed-upstroke birds such as swallows show a non-sinusoidal tip path, with a pause near the body in mid-upstroke.

### Cited Findings
- **House martin [WT]**:

| Speed | Wingbeat frequency | Downstroke ratio τ | Span ratio | Amplitude/chord |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 m/s | 11 Hz | 0.41 | 0.39 | 2.5 |
| 6 m/s | 10.7 Hz | 0.38 | 0.41 | 2.5 |
| 8 m/s | 10 Hz | 0.34 | 0.33 | 2.6 |
| 10 m/s | 9.1 Hz | 0.32 | 0.31 | 2.7 |

  Downstroke duration stays about 0.035 s at all speeds, while the upstroke lengthens from about 0.06 to 0.08 s. Frequency drops about 14% from 4 to 10 m/s, and amplitude rises about 10%. [Rosén, Spedding & Hedenström 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)
- House martin wingtip path: at 8 and 10 m/s the wingtips pause in mid-upstroke, held close to the body. The tip trajectory fits a two-frequency Fourier series, and the second harmonic grows at higher speeds. The stroke-plane angle changes by about 16 degrees and the body angle by about 8 degrees across 4–10 m/s. [Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391) [WT]
- **Pigeon, slow flight [FF-lab]**: wingbeat frequency 6.67 Hz, downstroke about 0.067 s, upstroke about 0.083 s. [Spedding, Rayner & Pennycuick 1984, "Momentum and energy in the wake of a pigeon in slow flight"](https://typeset.io/pdf/momentum-and-energy-in-the-wake-of-a-pigeon-columba-livia-in-4nt38b6vbe.pdf) (snippet only)
- **Hummingbirds**: the lateral-view wing path changes from a figure-8 in hovering to an ellipse in forward flight. [Tobalske et al. 2007, J Exp Biol 210:2368 (PDF)](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/210/13/2368/1539805/2368.pdf) (snippet only) [WT]
- Hovering hummingbirds hold the body at about 45 degrees and move the wings in a horizontal figure-8 (an "eight" lying on its side). [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
- In rufous hummingbirds the wingtip and wrist paths shift gradually with speed, as in other pointed-wing birds. [Tobalske et al. 2007](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/three-dimensional-kinematics-of-hummingbird-flight/) [WT]
- **Chukar ascending flight [FF-lab]**: the fingertip path angle in lateral view (gravitational frame) is 138.5 ± 4.6 degrees in ascending flight and 106.5 ± 3.7 degrees in WAIR. Trajectory angle is 59.8 degrees and body pitch 53.4 degrees. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)
- In a pigeon model, timing was parameterized with mid-downstroke at phase 0.25 and the start of the upstroke at 0.5, which assumes τ = 0.5. Peak pronation was placed at mid-downstroke through a π/2 phase lag. [Parslew 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085) [model]

### Inferences
- In these wind-tunnel and slow-flight data the upstroke is often longer than the downstroke: τ = 0.45 in pigeon slow flight and 0.32–0.41 in the house martin. A sensible animation default is τ of about 0.4–0.5, reduced at higher cruising speed. Keep the downstroke duration nearly constant and slow the upstroke as speed rises, as the house martin does.
- **Side view (body frame) for level forward flight**: the tip traces a tilted, elongated loop or ellipse. The downstroke runs down and forward, and the upstroke runs up and back, closer to the body. In flexed-upstroke birds the upstroke arc is much shorter and passes near the body, so the loop is asymmetric. A fundamental plus a second harmonic reproduces the observed mid-upstroke pause. A figure-8 is characteristic of hovering (hummingbirds, and slow tip-reversal flight); it opens into an ellipse as speed rises.
- **Front view**: the downstroke sweeps a wide arc at full span. On the upstroke the tip path moves inboard because span drops, to about 0.3–0.4 of full span in swallows. In the earth frame the tip traces a sinusoid-like wave, and the troughs are wider than the crests because the wing is extended on the downstroke.

### Gaps
- No full-text downstroke-ratio values were retrieved for pigeons in level cruising flight, or for large birds (geese, raptors) and corvids.
- Front-view tip-path figures were not available in text form, so the front-view description above is an inference from span ratios and joint data.

## Q3. Wing twist, angle of attack, pronation/supination and stroke-plane angle through the cycle

### Takeaway
The humerus pronates (leading edge down) during the downstroke and supinates on the upstroke. In chukars, supination starts around mid-downstroke and the long-axis rotation spans about 36 degrees. In tip-reversal flight the distal wing supinates strongly at mid-upstroke. Measured angles of attack in that posture are high: about 46 degrees at mid-downstroke and about 66 degrees at mid-upstroke in pigeons. The stroke plane tilts with speed, by about 16 degrees across 4–10 m/s in house martins. Hummingbirds change stroke-plane angle, body angle, chord angle and angle of attack with speed while holding frequency constant.

### Cited Findings
- Chukar humeral long-axis rotation (pronation/supination) range: 35.8 ± 15.1 degrees. Supination begins at about 29% of the wingstroke (mid-downstroke), with a second pronation phase during the upstroke in ascending flight. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074) [FF-lab]
- Tip-reversal upstroke: the distal wing is **supinated at mid-upstroke**. The swept-wing upstroke used at higher speeds lacks this supination. [Tobalske, Hedrick & Biewener 2003](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/wing-kinematics-of-avian-flight-across-speeds/) [WT]
- Pigeon wings in tip-reversal postures were tested at in vivo angles of attack: **66 degrees at mid-upstroke and 46 degrees at mid-downstroke**. The upstroke wing still produced substantial force. [Crandell & Tobalske 2011, "Aerodynamics of tip-reversal upstroke in a revolving pigeon wing"](https://typeset.io/pdf/aerodynamics-of-tip-reversal-upstroke-in-a-revolving-pigeon-3d13rw0x24.pdf) (snippet only) [ex vivo, revolving wing]
- Downstroke twist: the leading edge of the distal wing is lower than the trailing edge, so the force on the hand tilts forward to give thrust, while the proximal wing gives mainly lift. [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
- Rufous hummingbird [WT]: body angle and stroke-plane angle change with speed, but wingbeat frequency does not. Chord angle, angle of attack, wingbeat amplitude, wing angular velocity and mid-downstroke span also change with speed. [Tobalske et al. 2007](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/three-dimensional-kinematics-of-hummingbird-flight/)
- Hummingbirds partially invert their wings on each stroke, so the airfoil stays correctly oriented for lift in both directions. [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
- House martin [WT]: the stroke-plane angle changes by about 16 degrees and the body angle by about 8 degrees from 4 to 10 m/s. Absolute values are given only in the figures. [Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)
- Pigeon power-optimal model [model]:
  - The stroke-plane angle γ comes out at about −45 degrees near 7 m/s, and is shallower in steeper climbs.
  - Pronation amplitude was allowed from 0 to 90 degrees, with peak pronation at mid-downstroke.
  - The optimizer chose an elevation amplitude Φ of 36 degrees, within an allowed range of 0–75 degrees, for the minimum-power stiff-wing mode at 5.1 Hz.
  - The minimum-power cruise speed is 13.6 m/s.
  - [Parslew 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085)
- Shoulder roll and wrist roll are coupled during the pigeon upstroke. [Shen et al. 2024](https://mdpi.com/2313-7673/9/9/555) (snippet only) [FF-lab]

### Inferences
- **Twist schedule for animation**:
  - *Downstroke*: pronate the hand, with more nose-down twist toward the tip than at the root (washout). Peak pronation is around mid-downstroke, and the outer wing has a forward-tilted chord so it acts as a propeller.
  - *Bottom of the stroke*: begin supinating from about mid-to-late downstroke.
  - *Upstroke, slow flight*: strong supination of the hand, so the dorsal surface of the primaries faces forward and down during the backward "flick".
  - *Upstroke, fast flight*: little supination. The hand stays near zero geometric angle of attack and is swept back.
- The stroke plane should tilt further from vertical, toward horizontal relative to the earth, as speed falls and the body pitches up. In hovering hummingbirds the stroke plane is nearly horizontal and the body is pitched up about 45 degrees. As speed rises, body and stroke plane rotate toward the flight path.

### Gaps
- No numeric spanwise twist distribution (root versus tip geometric angle of attack over time) was found for a bird in level flight.
- In Parslew 2015 the sign convention for the stroke-plane angle γ, and whether Φ is a semi-amplitude, were not stated in the extracted text. Treat −45 degrees and 36 degrees as model outputs, not measurements.
- The abstract of Crandell & Tobalske 2015 ("Kinematics and aerodynamics of avian upstrokes", J Exp Biol 218:2518), likely the best source here, was blocked (403).

## Q4. Primary feather behavior and the "tip-reversal" upstroke

### Takeaway
On the downstroke the primaries overlap into a closed, continuous airfoil. On the upstroke they separate into slots that let air pass through. In the tip-reversal upstroke, used at low speed by pointed-wing birds, the humerus elevates, the primaries rotate (supinate) to open gaps, and then sweep upward and backward as the wrist extends. The wingtip effectively reverses direction and can still produce useful aerodynamic force, roughly 14–27% of downstroke force. Rounded-wing birds such as magpies instead "feather" a flexed wing on every upstroke.

### Cited Findings
- **Tip-reversal sequence (cockatiel at 1 m/s)**, as summarized from Hedrick et al. 2004:
  1. The wing reverses direction and the humerus is elevated.
  2. The primaries rotate slightly, opening gaps between feathers.
  3. The rotated primaries sweep upward as the wrist extends.
  4. The primaries return to their normal orientation, and the wing moves forward and upward.

  [avesbiology.com, citing Hedrick, Usherwood & Biewener 2004 J Exp Biol 207:1689](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
- Accelerometer data from cockatiels suggest that the tip-reversal upstroke produces about **14% of the net force of the downstroke**. [Hedrick, Usherwood & Biewener 2004, J Exp Biol 207:1689](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/207/10/1689/1548750/1689.pdf) (snippet only) [WT]
- Pigeons are reported to generate significant upstroke lift, about **27% of downstroke lift**, with the wingtip-reversal upstroke. [Biewener lab, pigeon research summary](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/pigeon-turning.md) (snippet only; the page returned 403 when fetched)
- In the revolving pigeon-wing study, wings in mid-upstroke tip-reversal posture produced substantial force at an angle of attack of 66 degrees. [Crandell & Tobalske 2011](https://typeset.io/pdf/aerodynamics-of-tip-reversal-upstroke-in-a-revolving-pigeon-3d13rw0x24.pdf) (snippet only) [ex vivo]
- Speed threshold: pointed-wing species (budgerigar, cockatiel, ringed turtle dove) use tip reversal at **≤ 7–9 m/s**, and a swept-wing upstroke without supination above that. Magpies use a **flexed-wing, feathered upstroke** at all steady speeds. [Tobalske, Hedrick & Biewener 2003](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/wing-kinematics-of-avian-flight-across-speeds/) [WT]
- On the upstroke the tips of the primaries separate, creating slots that let air through. [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
- In a pigeon model, feather angles were set proportional to wing extension, so feathers align with the chord when the wing is fully flexed. This is a qualitative model assumption, not a measurement. [Parslew 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085) [model]
- Feathers can be fanned or folded passively by skeletal motion. Stanford's PigeonBot actuates 40 elastically connected real pigeon feathers using only 4 servo-driven wrist and finger joints (42 DOF total), relying on the coupled elbow–wrist mechanism. [TechCrunch on Chang et al. 2020 (Lentink lab)](https://techcrunch.com/2020/01/16/pigeonbot-brings-flying-robots-closer-to-real-birds) (popular press)
- Upstroke force varies with speed. In the house martin at 4 m/s, the upstroke contributes essentially nothing to weight support, and the downstroke produces a compact, strong start vortex. The upstroke's contribution grows with speed. [Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391) [WT]
- Hummingbirds, by contrast, produce about 75% of their lift on the downstroke and 25% on the upstroke. In Ruby-throated hummingbirds the downstroke's vertical force is about 2.5 times that of the upstroke. [avesbiology.com, citing Warrick et al. 2005 Nature and Song et al. 2014 J R Soc Interface](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)

### Inferences
- **Feather states to animate**:
  - *Downstroke*: primaries fully overlapped and fanned to the maximum span. The tips bend upward under load. Tip bending is commonly seen in footage but was not quantified in the sources found.
  - *Upstroke (all styles)*: primaries rotate about their own shafts, so each feather's leading edge goes down relative to the airflow, and gaps open between the outer 4–6 primaries. ("Feathering" here means each feather rotates edge-on, like an oar blade.)
  - *Tip-reversal upstroke*: the whole hand supinates and flicks up and backward, then re-pronates late in the upstroke.
  - *Flexed upstroke*: the primaries slide under one another as the wrist folds, so the hand shortens.
- A simple driver is to tie primary spread and rotation to wrist extension and hand supination. This follows the biological coupling: feather fanning is largely passive, following the skeleton (Stowers et al.; PigeonBot).

### Gaps
- No quantitative data were found on slot gap widths, the number of separated primaries, or primary bending and twist angles over the stroke.
- Lentink-lab work on feather-overlap adhesion ("directional fasteners") and on feather flex was not retrieved.
- "Feathered upstroke" is used as a term in Tobalske et al. 2003, but its precise definition and supporting images were not retrieved.

## Q5. Quantitative scaling: wingbeat frequency vs mass, stroke amplitude, span ratio and Strouhal number

### Takeaway
Wingbeat frequency falls with body mass. Cruising frequency scales roughly as m^−1/6, while maximum (takeoff) frequency scales nearer m^−1/3. Pennycuick's empirical formula is f = 1.08 · m^(1/3) · g^(1/2) · b^(−1) · S^(−1/4) · ρ^(−1/3). Cruising birds operate at Strouhal numbers of about 0.2–0.4, clustered near the low end (St ≈ 0.21 for continuous flappers and 0.25 for intermittent flappers). Stroke angle scales as about 67 · b^(−0.24) degrees, with b in metres. Span ratios range from about 0.3 (swallow, flexed upstroke) to about 0.93 (hummingbird).

### Cited Findings

**Wingbeat frequency**
- Pennycuick (1990) fitted a formula to field observations of 32 bird species, combining multiple regression with dimensional analysis: **f = 1.08 · m^(1/3) · g^(1/2) · b^(−1) · S^(−1/4) · ρ^(−1/3)** (m = mass, b = span, S = wing area, ρ = air density). [Pennycuick 1990, J Exp Biol 150:171](https://next.sciencegate.app/document/10.1242/jeb.150.1.171); [PDF](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/150/1/171/2640396/jexbio_150_1_171.pdf) [FF-field]
- Pennycuick (1996) re-fitted the exponents using 15 additional species, adding a moment-of-inertia term and a density exponent of −3/8. [Pennycuick 1996, J Exp Biol 199:1613](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/wingbeat-frequency-birds-steady-cruising-flight-new-data-improved-predictions) (snippet only; exact revised exponents not verified, see Gaps)
- A radar and cine study of 153 Western Palaearctic species plus 2 African species found that the general model explained most of the variation, with coefficients only slightly different from Pennycuick's. For passerine-type flight, mass and span made no significant contribution, because mass, span and area are strongly collinear. [Search summary; apparently Bruderer et al. 2010, Ibis](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/wing-beat-characteristics-birds-recorded-tracking-radar-cine-camera) (snippet only; mapping of snippet to this record not fully verified) [FF-field]
- **Albatrosses and petrels [FF-field, animal-borne accelerometers, 26 birds, 0.6–9.4 kg]**:
  - Takeoff ("high") frequency: HF = 6.1 · m^(−0.30), 95% CI −0.33 to −0.27. Predicted exponent −1/3, from maximum muscle power under geometric similarity.
  - Cruising ("low") frequency: LF = 3.9 · m^(−0.18), 95% CI −0.20 to −0.16, not significantly different from the −1/6 predicted for the minimum frequency needed to sustain flight.
  - Examples: streaked shearwater (0.6 kg) about 7.5 Hz at takeoff and 4.2 Hz cruising; white-chinned petrel (1.3 kg) 5.6 Hz and 3.9 Hz; wandering albatross (9.4 kg) 2.9–3.4 Hz at takeoff and 2.5–2.7 Hz cruising.

  [Sato et al. 2009, PLoS ONE 4:e5400](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2670537)
- Across birds in general, frequency is reported to scale as about **m^−0.27**, which is similar to free-flying bats (−0.26 to −0.30). [Norberg & Norberg 2012 (bat scaling paper), as summarized in search results](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/scaling-wingbeat-frequency-body-mass-bats-limits-maximum-bat-size) (snippet only; page 403)
- Example frequencies:
  - House martin: 9.1–11 Hz. [Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391) [WT]
  - Pigeon, slow flight: 6.67 Hz. [Spedding et al. 1984](https://typeset.io/pdf/momentum-and-energy-in-the-wake-of-a-pigeon-columba-livia-in-4nt38b6vbe.pdf) (snippet)
  - Ruby-throated hummingbird, hovering: about 40 Hz (females about 45 Hz). [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
  - Rufous hummingbird: frequency independent of speed from 0 to 12 m/s. [Tobalske et al. 2007](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/three-dimensional-kinematics-of-hummingbird-flight/) [WT]
- In a pigeon model, frequency was bounded at 3–9 Hz, which is 0.5–1.5 times the allometric prediction, implying a prediction of about 6 Hz. The minimum-power stiff-wing solution was 5.1 Hz, and flexing modes exceeded 8 Hz. [Parslew 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085) [model]

**Strouhal number and stroke amplitude**
- Propulsive efficiency peaks for **0.2 < St < 0.4**, where St = f·A/U (A = peak-to-peak tip excursion). Cruising birds, bats and insects converge on this range, but only when cruising. [Taylor, Nudds & Thomas 2003, Nature 425:707](https://www.biology.ox.ac.uk/node/1030831); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v425y2003i6959d10.1038_nature02000.html)
- Cruising birds sit at the low end of that range.
- **Stroke angle θ ≈ 67 · b^(−0.24)** degrees, with b in metres.
- Predicted frequency: **f ≈ St · U / (b · sin(33.5 · b^(−0.24)))**, with **St = 0.21 for continuous (direct) fliers and 0.25 for intermittent fliers**. This explained 90% of the variance in wingbeat frequency across 60 species.
- [Nudds, Taylor & Thomas 2004, Proc R Soc B 271:2071](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/oxford-uuid:7ebcc4ec-c34a-413c-b83d-6a60d3e8d0ef) (abstract only)
- Measured amplitudes:
  - Chukar ascending flight: humeral elevation/depression range 101.6 degrees. [Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074) [FF-lab]
  - House martin: tip amplitude/chord of 2.5–2.7, rising about 10% with speed. [Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391) [WT]

**Span ratio (upstroke span / downstroke span)**
- House martin: 0.39 (4 m/s), 0.41 (6 m/s), 0.33 (8 m/s), 0.31 (10 m/s). This decrease with speed is unusual; other species show R rising with speed. [Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391) [WT]
- Rufous hummingbird: average of about **0.93**. [Tobalske et al. 2007](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/three-dimensional-kinematics-of-hummingbird-flight/) [WT]
- Pointed-wing species (budgerigar, cockatiel, dove): wingspan > wrist span at mid-upstroke at all speeds. Magpie: wrist span ≥ wingspan, meaning the hand is folded in. [Tobalske et al. 2003](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/wing-kinematics-of-avian-flight-across-speeds/) [WT]
- Pigeon model: hand-to-whole-wing length ratio of 0.74, wing length 0.32 m. [Parslew 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085)

**Intermittent flight (context)**
- Flap-bounding (folded-wing pauses) is not seen in birds heavier than about 300 g. Flap-gliding is more efficient at low speeds and flap-bounding at high speeds. Budgerigars flex their wings during non-flapping phases. [avesbiology.com, citing Tobalske & Dial 1994 and Tobalske et al. 1999](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)

### Inferences
- **Isometric check**: if b ∝ m^(1/3) and S ∝ m^(2/3), Pennycuick's 1990 formula gives f ∝ m^(1/3 − 1/3 − 1/6) = **m^(−1/6)**. This matches the cruising exponent of −0.18 from Sato et al. The steeper −0.27 to −0.33 exponents describe maximum or takeoff frequencies, or broad interspecific datasets in which wings are not geometrically similar.
- **Worked example: pigeon**, assuming m = 0.4 kg (Parslew's value), b ≈ 0.65 m, S ≈ 0.06 m², sea-level ρ = 1.225 kg/m³. The morphology values are assumptions, not sourced.
  - Pennycuick 1990 gives f ≈ 1.08 × 0.737 × 3.13 × 1.54 × 2.02 × 0.935 ≈ **7.2 Hz**.
  - Nudds et al. 2004 at U = 13.6 m/s (Parslew's minimum-power speed) and St = 0.21: θ ≈ 74 degrees, θ/2 ≈ 37 degrees, A ≈ 0.65 × sin 37° ≈ 0.39 m, so f ≈ 0.21 × 13.6 / 0.39 ≈ **7.3 Hz**.
  - The two independent models agree.
- **Stroke angle from Nudds' fit**:

| Span b | Example | θ (peak-to-peak) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.25 m | small passerine | ≈ 93° |
| 0.65 m | pigeon | ≈ 74° |
| 2.3 m | swan | ≈ 55° |
| 3.0 m | albatross | ≈ 51° |

  Small birds flap through larger arcs. Takeoff and slow flight use much larger amplitudes, around 100 degrees or more at the shoulder in chukar ascending flight.
- **Animation default parameter set (cruising, mid-size bird)**:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Frequency | from Pennycuick or Nudds |
| Stroke angle | 67·b^(−0.24) degrees |
| Downstroke fraction τ | about 0.4–0.5 |
| Span ratio, flexed-upstroke birds | 0.3–0.5 |
| Span ratio, pointed-wing fast fliers | about 0.6–0.8 (inferred; not directly sourced) |
| Span ratio, hummingbird | about 0.93 |
| St | about 0.2–0.25 |

  For slow flight and takeoff, raise frequency toward the takeoff allometry, raise amplitude toward 100 degrees or more, and switch to tip reversal for pointed wings below about 7–9 m/s.

### Gaps
- The exact exponents of Pennycuick's 1996 revision could not be verified. The often-quoted form from *Modelling the Flying Bird* (2008) is f = m^(3/8) g^(1/2) b^(−23/24) S^(−1/3) ρ^(−3/8), but it was **not confirmed** in any source fetched this session; a search snippet suggested a different span exponent. The report writer should treat it as unverified.
- No sourced numeric span ratios were found for pigeons, cockatiels or doves at specific speeds. Tobalske & Dial 1996 and Hedrick et al. 2002 hold these but were blocked.
- No Strouhal values for individual species were retrieved, only the range and the fitted constants.

## Q6. The muscles that drive each phase, and how they shape the motion

### Takeaway
The pectoralis, the largest flight muscle, depresses and pronates the humerus to power the downstroke. Its peak force falls in the first half of the downstroke. The supracoracoideus elevates and supinates the humerus. Its peak force falls at the downstroke-to-upstroke transition rather than mid-upstroke, so it both brakes the wing at the bottom of the stroke and starts the upstroke. Its power output roughly matches the inertial cost of raising the wing. The distal muscles (biceps and humerotriceps at the elbow, plus the wrist muscles) shape the wing for each phase.

### Cited Findings
- The pectoralis is the main downstroke muscle and the supracoracoideus the upstroke muscle. The supracoracoideus is relatively larger in hummingbirds. Pectoralis-to-supracoracoideus mass ratios are about 2:1 to 3:1 in hummingbirds, auks and penguins (wing-propelled divers that use the upstroke heavily). [avesbiology.com, citing Deeming 2023 J Zool](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)
- Peak pectoralis force occurs in the **first half of the downstroke**. [Biewener et al. 1998, as cited in Robertson & Biewener 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-f736-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content) (snippet only)
- **Supracoracoideus (pigeon)**: its primary role is to elevate the humerus and rotate it into supination. It was hypothesized, and then supported, that its peak force falls at the **downstroke-to-upstroke transition**, not mid-upstroke. Its length and activation patterns resemble the pectoralis's but are shifted in time. Earlier EMG work suggested it decelerates the wing late in the downstroke and accelerates it early in the upstroke. Its mechanical power output matched the estimated inertial power of the upstroke. [Tobalske & Biewener 2008, J Exp Biol 211:170 (Biewener lab page)](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/contractile-properties-pigeon-supracoracoideus-during-different-modes-flight); [abstract](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4NE1rYx7); [review excerpt](https://core.ac.uk/download/28945437.pdf) (abstract/snippet only)
- Pigeon fascicle strain rates in the pectoralis and the **biceps** (an elbow flexor) are higher during takeoff than during mid-flight or landing. [Robertson & Biewener 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-f736-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content) (snippet only) [FF-lab]
- In rufous hummingbirds the supracoracoideus deactivates **before** the upstroke begins, which differs from other birds. ["Effects of flight speed upon muscle activity in hummingbirds" (Tobalske et al.)](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_733457876) (abstract only)
- At slow speeds wing inertia dominates power cost: over 34 W at 6 m/s versus 8.7 W without wing mass in a 0.4 kg pigeon model. This explains why upstroke muscle work largely goes into accelerating and decelerating the wing. [Parslew 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085) [model]
- In many small birds the pectoralis has a single fiber type, which limits its useful range of strain rates. This is one explanation for intermittent flap-bounding, as a way to modulate power. [avesbiology.com](https://avesbiology.com/554notes3.html) (secondary)

### Inferences
- **How muscle timing shapes the motion**:
  - The pectoralis peaks early in the downstroke, so the wing accelerates fastest early and is near peak angular velocity around mid-downstroke.
  - The supracoracoideus engages at the bottom of the stroke, braking the wing and reversing it, so the turnaround is quick and short rather than a lingering pause.
  - The upstroke is driven by a smaller muscle with less power, which fits a longer, slower upstroke (τ < 0.5) and a folded wing that cuts the moment of inertia.
- **For animation easing**:
  - Use a fast-in ease at the start of the downstroke, with peak angular velocity near mid-downstroke.
  - Make the bottom reversal sharp, with the proximal-to-distal lag described in Q1.
  - Ease out the top reversal more softly, since peak elevation comes at about 68% of the upstroke in chukars, followed by a short dwell before the next downstroke.

### Gaps
- No quantitative EMG onset and offset times (as % of cycle), or pectoralis strain values (for example, about 30% strain is often quoted for pigeons), could be verified from fetched text. The Biewener and Tobalske primary papers on silverchair returned 403.
- The search summary also stated that "pectoralis power was 42–46% of the estimated aerodynamic requirement". It came from an abstract aggregator, and its context (which species, which flight mode) could not be confirmed, so it is excluded.
- Activity timing of the humerotriceps, scapulohumeralis and wrist muscles was not found. An older source to consult is Dial, Goslow & Jenkins 1988, "A functional analysis of the primary upstroke and downstroke muscles in the domestic pigeon during flight" (J Exp Biol 134:1), which was identified only by title.
