# Bird wing motion across flight phases and speeds (takeoff, acceleration, climbing, cruising, gliding, intermittent flight, descending, landing)

Notes on sources: Berg & Biewener 2010 (pigeon takeoff/landing), Tobalske 2007 (JEB review) and Rosén & Hedenström 2001 (jackdaw gliding) were read in full from PDF. All other items come from abstracts, search snippets or secondary summaries, and are flagged where that matters. Wingbeat frequency is WBF.

## 1. Takeoff: how do the first wingbeats differ, what do the legs contribute, how many beats to steady flight?

### Takeaway
The legs launch the bird. At the moment the feet leave the perch, a pigeon already has about 74% of the velocity it will have at the next upstroke-downstroke transition. The first 2–4 wingbeats then have the highest frequency, the largest amplitude and the fastest downstroke of the whole flight, with a steeply downward-tilted stroke plane and a body held nearly flat or nose-down. All of these values decay beat by beat toward cruise values, and steady flight is not yet reached after 4 beats.

### Cited Findings
**Pigeon (Columba livia, 431 ± 67 g, perch-to-perch flights over 6–8 m, 250 fps; Berg & Biewener 2010)**
- **Leg contribution.** When the feet broke contact with the perch, early in the first downstroke, the birds already had 74 ± 10% of the velocity they reached by the next upstroke-downstroke transition. The authors say "most of the acceleration into flight is the result of push-off from the legs," and over the four wingbeats analysed the legs supplied about one quarter of the acceleration and the wings the rest. — [Berg & Biewener 2010, JEB 213:1651](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Other species.** At ground departure, starlings (Sturnus vulgaris) and quail (Coturnix coturnix) already have 80–90% of the velocity they reach by the following upstroke-downstroke transition (Earls 2000). Hummingbirds taking off from a perch have only 59% (Tobalske et al. 2004). — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Velocity.** Horizontal velocity was 1.52 ± 0.23 m/s at the start of wingbeat 1 and 4.51 ± 0.48 m/s at the end of wingbeat 4. It was still rising, so steady cruise had not been reached after 4 beats. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Peak acceleration.** The largest horizontal acceleration came in wingbeat 2, at **9.14 ± 2.22 m/s²**, not wingbeat 1, and it fell in later beats. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Wingbeat frequency.** WBF fell steadily during takeoff, from **8.88 ± 0.28 Hz (beat 1) to 7.68 ± 0.56 Hz (beat 4)**. Mid-flight WBF was **6.57 ± 0.30 Hz**, so beat 1 is about 35% above cruise. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Amplitude.** Downstroke sweep angle, an amplitude measure that includes path curvature, was largest in the first two takeoff beats at **203 ± 5°**, against **176 ± 17°** mid-flight. In takeoff the wingtips often cross at the top and bottom of the stroke. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Downstroke angular velocity.** Downstroke sweep velocity fell from **3.16 ± 0.16 °/ms (beat 1) to 2.51 ± 0.23 °/ms (beat 4)**. The authors conclude that "downstroke velocity must be greater for accelerating flight." — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Stroke plane.** The local stroke plane angle was tilted steeply downward and rose from **−60.8 ± 5.3° (beat 1) to −47.5 ± 1.0° (beat 4)**, against **−29.5 ± 5.5°** in mid-flight. The steep tilt pushes air rearward and so produces forward thrust. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Body angle.** Body angle at mid-downstroke was **−13.0 ± 7.9° in beat 1**, with the shoulder below the rump (head-down or flat), against **19.0 ± 9.3°** in mid-flight. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Wingtip path.** In side view the wingtip traced a **figure-of-eight** relative to the shoulder in most takeoff and landing beats, but mostly a loop around the shoulder in mid-flight. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Tail.** The tail stayed broadly spread throughout (mean 151 ± 18°) and did not narrow significantly during takeoff. Tail angle at mid-downstroke was 4.4 ± 9.3° in beat 1. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Earlier pigeon work.** Dial (1992) found WBF similar in level flight and landing but higher in takeoff, and amplitude higher in both takeoff and landing than in level flight. — cited in [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)

**Other species and scaling**
- **Zebra finch and diamond dove** (Provini et al. 2012). The first wingbeat has a "transitional role" in force production, and the wings contribute relatively little to the initial take-off. A follow-up paper by the same group says hindlimbs supplied about 95% of the take-off contribution over roughly three wingbeats. — [Provini et al. 2012, JEB](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/transition-from-leg-to-wing-forces-during-take-off-in-birds/); 95% figure from the [Provini et al. 2014 landing paper](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/15/2659/12207/Transition-from-wing-to-leg-forces-during-landing). This is a snippet only. It conflicts with the pigeon figure of legs supplying about 25% of acceleration over 4 beats, probably because the two studies define the contribution and the time window differently.
- **Initial velocity vs body size.** Initial flight velocity increases with body size, from **0.8 m/s in the rufous hummingbird (3 g) to 6 m/s in the wild turkey (6 kg)**. — [Tobalske 2007, JEB 210:3135](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Takeoff power.** Muscle mass-specific mechanical power is **60–150 W/kg in cruising flight and about 400 W/kg in take-off** (blue-breasted quail). — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Power vs mass.** Mass-specific whole-body power during escape takeoff falls as body size increases. In galliforms it scales with WBF, which is expected to scale roughly as mass^−1/3. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Takeoff upstroke in rounded wings.** Galliform birds have rounded wings but use a **tip-reversal upstroke during take-off**, while in level flight rounded-winged birds usually use a flexed upstroke. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Soaring birds.** Andean condors flapped only about 1% of flight time. More than 75% of all flapping was associated with takeoffs, and the rest happened close to the ground. The authors conclude that the overall flight effort of the largest soaring birds is set by the demands of takeoff. — [Williams et al. 2020, PNAS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PNAS..11717884W); [sci.news summary](https://www.sci.news/biology/andean-condor-flight-08638.html)

### Inferences
- For a pigeon-like bird, a reasonable takeoff profile is a leg push to about 1.5 m/s, then about 4 beats with WBF decaying linearly from about 1.35× to about 1.17× cruise WBF. Over the same beats, amplitude goes from about 1.15× cruise toward cruise, stroke plane from about −60° to −47° (cruise −30°), and body pitch from about −13° to near level. Peak acceleration (about 0.9 g horizontal) comes on beat 2.
- Because the stroke plane stays at a nearly fixed angle relative to the body (Section 9), most of the change in stroke-plane orientation during takeoff can be produced by pitching the body.

### Gaps
- No source gave a definitive "number of beats until steady state". In the pigeons, WBF was still above cruise and velocity still rising at beat 4. I found no data on beats 5 onward.
- I could not access full-text numbers from Provini et al. 2012 for zebra finch or diamond dove takeoff WBF and amplitude, or from Earls 2000 or Tobalske & Dial 2000 (galliforms).

## 2. Wingbeat frequency and amplitude vs flight speed; the U-shaped power curve

### Takeaway
Mechanical and metabolic power follow a U-shaped curve against speed, with a minimum-power speed (Vmp) and a faster maximum-range speed (Vmr). Wingbeat frequency, however, is fairly flat across steady speeds in many species, or only weakly U-shaped, and its minimum need not coincide with the power minimum. Birds change power mainly through muscle force and recruitment, amplitude, stroke plane, upstroke posture, body angle and intermittent pauses rather than through frequency. In the takeoff data above, frequency and amplitude both rise when birds accelerate. Across steady speeds, the stroke plane becomes more vertical and wing excursion grows with speed in several species, while body angle falls.

### Cited Findings
- **Theory.** Every aerodynamic model predicts a U-shaped curve: induced power (the cost of lift) falls with speed, profile and parasite power rise, and the curve has a characteristic Vmp and Vmr. Ecological models often assume birds fly at Vmp when foraging and at Vmr when migrating. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Measured power curves.** In vivo pectoralis power (strain gauges plus sonomicrometry) is U-shaped in the dove (Zenaida macroura), cockatiel (Nymphicus hollandicus) and black-billed magpie (Pica hudsonia) over about 0–14 m/s. Read approximately from Fig. 2B of the review:
  - Cockatiel falls from about 140 W/kg at 0 m/s to about 75 W/kg at about 5–7 m/s, then rises steeply to about 230 W/kg at about 13 m/s.
  - Dove runs from about 125 W/kg at its minimum (about 7 m/s) to about 235 W/kg at about 14 m/s.
  - Magpie is flat at about 60–75 W/kg across about 4–12 m/s.
  - [Tobalske 2007 Fig. 2](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight), from [Tobalske et al. 2003, Nature 421:363](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/comparative-power-curves-bird-flight).
- **Curve shape.** The cockatiel curve is "acutely concave", while the dove's is intermediate in shape with higher mass-specific power at most speeds. **WBF and mechanical power do not necessarily share minima.** — [Tobalske et al. 2003, Nature](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/comparative-power-curves-bird-flight)
- **Metabolic power.** Metabolic power (oxygen consumption) is also U-shaped in cockatiels, with a minimum near about 9–10 m/s in Fig. 2C, and muscular efficiency is about 20%. Vmp and Vmr are faster when measured from metabolic power than from muscle power. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **How power is modulated.** Across speeds, cockatiels change muscle power mainly by recruiting a different share of pectoralis motor units, which changes force. Pigeons similarly vary recruitment and pectoralis force between flight modes. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Magpie and pigeon.** In steady flight, **WBF did not change appreciably with speed** in magpies (4–14 m/s) or pigeons (6–20 m/s). **Body angle fell** as speed rose. — [Tobalske & Dial 1996, JEB 199:263](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/199/2/263/7465/Flight-Kinematics-of-Black-Billed-Magpies-and) (abstract and secondary summaries); also [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Multi-species stroke plane and excursion.** In budgerigars, cockatiels, ring-neck doves and black-billed magpies, the **stroke plane becomes more vertical and wing excursion increases with speed** (Tobalske, Hedrick & Biewener 2003, J Avian Biol 34:177). — cited in [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Thrush nightingale differs.** In the thrush nightingale (Luscinia luscinia), the wingtip path steepens as speed decreases, and **wingbeat amplitude does not depend on speed** (Rosén et al. 2004). — cited in [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Barn swallow.** WBF is **U-shaped** with airspeed over 4–14 m/s, with minima at about **8.9 and 8.7 m/s** in the two birds. Upstroke pauses (flap-gliding) at intermediate and high speeds lower the apparent WBF. — [Park, Rosén & Hedenström 2001, JEB 204:2741](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-pdf/204/15/2741/1238033/2741.pdf) (abstract)
- **Swallow vs magpie and pigeon.** Swallows show a frequency trough near the middle of their speed range, while magpies and pigeons show near-constant frequency. This is a real difference between species.
- **Strouhal number.** During cruising, a diverse set of birds (and insects and fish) keep the Strouhal number, frequency × amplitude ÷ forward speed, in the range **0.2–0.4**. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight), citing Taylor, Nudds & Thomas 2003, Nature 425:707
- **Frequency vs amplitude (secondary summary).** A 2022 synthesis of pigeon and other studies reports that pigeon **WBF did not vary significantly across ascending, level and descending flight**, while **wingbeat amplitude increased with induced power, climb rate, and accelerating flight**. Frequency and amplitude are described as only partial determinants of power. — [Krishnan et al. 2022 preprint, "The role of wingbeat frequency and amplitude in flight power"](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.28.497935v1.full) (published in [J R Soc Interface 19:20220168](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsif/article/19/193/20220168/90211/The-role-of-wingbeat-frequency-and-amplitude-in)). Full text not accessed; numbers unverified.
- **Bar-headed geese, wild.** WBF was tightly regulated within a flight, varying by only about **0.6 beats/s**, but a **5% rise in WBF went with a 19% rise in heart rate and an estimated 41% rise in flight power**. WBF rose as air density fell at altitude. — [Bishop et al. 2015, Science, via EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/570597); [Bangor record](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/the-roller-coaster-flight-strategy-of-bar-headed-geese-conserves-/)
- **Predicting cruise WBF from morphology.** Pennycuick's empirical formula is **f = 1.08 · m^(1/3) · g^(1/2) · b^(−1) · S^(−1/4) · ρ^(−1/3)** (m mass, b span, S wing area, ρ air density). In 1996 the span and area exponents were revised to −17/24 and −1/3. A tracking-radar test on 153 species found the general model explained most of the variation in WBF. — [Pennycuick 1990, JEB 150:171](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/150/1/171/5695/Predicting-Wingbeat-Frequency-and-Wavelength-of); [Pennycuick 1996, JEB 199:1613](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/199/7/1613/3391219/jexbio_199_7_1613.pdf); [tracking radar study](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/wing-beat-characteristics-birds-recorded-tracking-radar-cine-camera)

### Inferences
- For animation, hold cruise WBF roughly constant (within about ±10%) across steady speeds, or give it a shallow U with its minimum near the mid-range speed (swallow-like). Express speed through stroke-plane tilt, body pitch (decreasing with speed) and upstroke posture (Section 3), with amplitude rising modestly at the slow and fast extremes and sharply when accelerating.
- If the Strouhal number stays between 0.2 and 0.4 while frequency is nearly fixed, then frequency × amplitude must scale roughly with speed in cruise. In practice birds also manage this through flapping duty cycle (Section 5) and pitch, so amplitude need not scale linearly with speed.
- Departures in WBF seem to be reserved for unsteady phases: takeoff (+35%), landing (+18% at beat −1), climbing or thin air (small increases, but costly).

### Gaps
- I could not get exact WBF and amplitude tables vs speed for magpies and pigeons (Tobalske & Dial 1996) or cockatiels and doves (Tobalske et al. 2003, Hedrick et al. 2002), because the full texts were not accessible. Power values above are approximate figure readings.
- I found no source giving the actual Vmp and Vmr in m/s for these species. They appear only graphically.

## 3. Gaits: vortex-ring vs continuous-vortex, and how the upstroke changes with speed

### Takeaway
The classic two-gait scheme has a vortex-ring gait at slow speed, with an aerodynamically inactive, flexed or tip-reversal upstroke, and a continuous-vortex gait at fast speed, with an extended, lift-producing upstroke. It is now regarded as a simplification: kinematics and wakes change gradually with speed, and the upstroke produces some lift even at slow speeds. Wing shape determines the upstroke progression. Pointed, high-aspect-ratio wings (pigeon) go from tip-reversal (slow) to feathered (intermediate) to swept-wing (fast) upstroke. Rounded, low-aspect-ratio wings (magpie) use a flexed upstroke at all speeds.

### Cited Findings
- **Magpie and pigeon gaits.** Magpies used a vortex-ring gait at all steady speeds (4–14 m/s). Pigeons used a vortex-ring gait at **6 and 8 m/s**, a **transitional vortex-ring gait at 10 m/s**, and a **continuous-vortex gait at faster speeds** (to 20 m/s). Both species alternated steady flight with bouts of acceleration and deceleration, especially at intermediate speeds. — [Tobalske & Dial 1996](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/199/2/263/7465/Flight-Kinematics-of-Black-Billed-Magpies-and) (abstract via search summary)
- **Upstroke progression in pointed wings.** Pigeons illustrate tip-reversal upstroke at **6 m/s**, feathered upstroke at **10 m/s** and swept-wing upstroke at **16 m/s**. Black-billed magpies use a flexed upstroke at 10 m/s and at all speeds. — [Tobalske 2007, Fig. 3](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Downstroke vs upstroke.** "Downstroke kinematics are relatively invariant, upstroke kinematics differ among species and according to flight speed." — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Span ratio.** Span ratio, mid-upstroke span ÷ mid-downstroke span, **generally decreases as flight speed increases**, but may increase with speed in some passerines. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Rounded-wing exceptions.** Magpies change upstroke posture according to acceleration and deceleration, and galliforms use tip-reversal upstrokes during takeoff. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Gradual, not categorical, change.** All measured wing kinematics and muscle activity change gradually across speeds. Thrush nightingale wakes measured with DPIV show the upstroke is aerodynamically active at slow, medium and fast speeds. There are cross-stream vortices at the ends of half-strokes in slower flight and throughout the wingbeat in fast flight. — [Tobalske 2007, Fig. 4](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight), citing Spedding, Rosén & Hedenström 2003
- **Older theory.** Vortex theory predicted lower induced power for the continuous-vortex gait, because the wake area is larger. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)

### Inferences
- For a high-aspect-ratio bird model, blend the upstroke from tip-reversal (wing strongly flexed, primaries rotated or supinated, tip flicked backward) at ≤ about 6–8 m/s, through feathered (partly flexed) around 10 m/s, to swept (wrist flexed, hand swept back but kept partly spread) at ≥ about 14–16 m/s. Lower the upstroke span ratio as speed rises.
- For a low-aspect-ratio bird (corvids, small passerines), keep a flexed upstroke at all speeds, with extra flexion while accelerating.
- If a single "gait switch" speed is needed, about 10 m/s for a pigeon-sized bird is the empirically documented transition.

### Gaps
- I found no numerical span-ratio-vs-speed values for flapping flight, only the qualitative trend.
- I did not find transition speeds for other species (cockatiel and dove from Hedrick et al. 2002) in accessible text.

## 4. Climbing vs level vs descending flight

### Takeaway
Direct kinematic numbers for steady climbing and descending were mostly out of reach. The available evidence says pigeons do not change WBF significantly between ascending, level and descending flight, and instead change amplitude with climb rate (secondary source). Slow descents use a more protracted wing posture with steep body angles. In wild geese, climbing and thin air raise WBF only a little, but small WBF increases are very costly.

### Cited Findings
- **Pigeon ascent and descent.** Berg & Biewener (2008, JEB 211:1120) tested whether climbing or descending power equals level-flight power plus the rate of change of potential energy. A secondary summary reports that pigeon **WBF did not vary significantly across ascending, level and descending flight**, while **amplitude increased with climb rate**. — [Krishnan et al. 2022 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.28.497935v1.full) and [Berg & Biewener 2008 record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5508830_Kinematics_and_power_requirements_of_ascending_and_descending_flight_in_the_pigeon_Columba_livia) (abstract not retrieved; unverified)
- **Slow descents.** A **more protracted wing posture** (wingstroke positioned further forward of the shoulder) is seen during slow descending flights of pigeons and at the end of landing. The authors suggest this moves the plane of force generation above the centre of mass and may improve stability at steep body angles. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Wingtip path in slow flight.** The figure-of-eight wingtip path seen in takeoff and landing is similar to that in slow ascending, level and descending pigeon flight. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Bar-headed geese.** WBF rose with altitude and falling air density but stayed within about 0.6 Hz in a flight, and +5% WBF went with +41% estimated power. The geese followed the terrain ("roller coaster") instead of holding high altitude, and occasionally used updrafts on windward valley walls. — [EurekAlert, Bishop et al. 2015](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/570597); [sci.news](https://www.sci.news/biology/science-bar-headed-geese-flying-himalayas-02410.html)
- **Distal wing muscles.** Distal wing muscles are activated mainly during non-level flight. Birds can fly steadily without them but cannot take off or land in a controlled way. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)

### Inferences
- For animation, express climbing as larger amplitude, with only a slight WBF increase (about 5–10%) and nose-up pitch. Express shallow descents as reduced amplitude or glides (Sections 5–6). Steep slow descents use high body angle, protracted wings and spread tail, resembling the landing posture in Section 8.

### Gaps
- I could not access Berg & Biewener 2008 for exact WBF, amplitude and stroke-plane values at the ascent and descent angles tested.
- I found no wind-tunnel climb data (tilted tunnel, flapping) with numbers.

## 5. Intermittent flight: flap-gliding and flap-bounding

### Takeaway
Many birds interrupt flapping with extended-wing glides (flap-gliding) or flexed-wing bounds, where the wings are folded against the body (flap-bounding). Theory says flap-gliding saves power mainly at slow-to-moderate speeds, and flap-bounding saves power at fast speeds above Vmr, helped by lift from the body. Small birds (<300 g, especially <30 g with rounded wings) bound. Birds over 300 g, such as pigeons, glide but never bound. The share of time spent flapping falls as speed rises, and in birds that use both modes the non-flapping phases shift from glides at slow speed to bounds at high speed.

### Cited Findings
- **Theory.** Compared with continuous flapping, flap-gliding needs less aerodynamic power at most speeds, especially slow ones, and flap-bounding saves power in fast flight above Vmr (Rayner 1985). Many species glide intermittently at slow speeds and switch to bounds or partial bounds at faster speeds. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Muscles.** The pectoralis and supracoracoideus are **inactive during bounds**, and make **isometric contractions with reduced activation during glides**, holding the wings out. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Body size and wing shape.**
  - Species that use both flap-gliding and flap-bounding are **<300 g or have pointed, high-aspect-ratio wings**.
  - Species **>300 g (e.g., pigeons) glide but do not bound**.
  - In passerines and woodpeckers the **percentage of time spent flapping increases with body mass**.
  - Small birds **<30 g with rounded, low-aspect-ratio wings almost exclusively bound**.
  - [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Budgerigar** (Melopsittacus undulatus, wind tunnel 7–16 m/s, Tobalske & Dial 1994). As speed increases, glides make up less of the non-flapping time and bounds more. Approximate readings from the bar chart:
  - 7 m/s: about 80% glides, about 15% partial bounds, about 5% bounds.
  - 11 m/s: about 18% glides, about 65% partial bounds.
  - 13–16 m/s: about 5–13% glides, about 35% partial bounds, about 55% bounds.
  - [Tobalske 2007, Fig. 5](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Zebra finch, wind tunnel** (Taeniopygia guttata, about 13–15 g, 0–14 m/s, 300 fps, 4 birds; Tobalske, Peacock & Dial 1999).
  - It **flap-bounded at all speeds**.
  - The flapping share of each cycle **fell as speed increased**.
  - A model indicated flap-bounding is advantageous over continuous flapping at **6–14 m/s**, with **body lift adding savings above about 7.1 m/s**.
  - Wing angular velocity changed with speed, contradicting the "fixed-gear" hypothesis that small birds can only flap one way.
  - [Tobalske et al. 1999, JEB 202:1725](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/202/13/1725/7978/Kinematics-of-flap-bounding-flight-in-the-zebra)
- **Bound duration and beats per burst.** In zebra finches both vary significantly with flight speed, which argues against a rigid central pattern generator. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Zebra finch flapping share.** The share of time flapping ranged from about **63% at 8 m/s to nearly 99% at 0 m/s** (hovering) in one post-surgery group. — [Tobalske et al. 2005, JEB 208:2895](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/208/15/2895/15765/Contractile-activity-of-the-pectoralis-in-the) (search snippet)
- **Free flight vs tunnel.** In free flight at about 6 m/s, zebra finches flapped about **50% of the time at about 28.5 Hz**, against about **69% at 24.3 Hz** in the wind tunnel. — [SICB abstract](https://sicb.org/abstracts/effect-of-distance-on-flap-bounding-flight-performance/)
- **Loaded vs unloaded finches.** Another study reports a mean flapping ratio of **0.675** in unloaded zebra finches. — [PMC6602328](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6602328/)
- **Body lift.** The body produces lift even with the wings fully folded during bounds. Including body and tail lift lowers the estimated power for fast flap-bounding flight. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Pigeons.** Pigeons **progressively flexed their wings during glides as flight speed increased but never performed bounds**. — [Tobalske & Dial 1996](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/199/2/263/7465/Flight-Kinematics-of-Black-Billed-Magpies-and) (via search summary)
- **Barn swallows.** Barn swallows insert upstroke pauses (short glides) at intermediate and high speeds. — [Park et al. 2001](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-pdf/204/15/2741/1238033/2741.pdf)
- **Why birds do it (open question).** Efficiency during slow flight in zebra finches is estimated at **11%**, against about 20% in fast-flying starlings. Why small birds bound even at slow speeds, where theory says bounding costs more, is unresolved. A leading hypothesis is that bounding is the only way small birds can modulate power, but zebra finches do modulate muscle contraction like larger birds. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)

### Inferences
- **Small finch-like bird (<30 g):** bursts of flapping separated by folded-wing ballistic bounds. Flapping share is about 100% at hover, about 65–70% at 6–8 m/s, and lower at 10–14 m/s. WBF in flapping bursts is high, about 24–29 Hz.
- **Mid-size bird (budgerigar or starling, 30–300 g):** glides at slow speed, partial bounds at intermediate speed, bounds at high speed.
- **Bird >300 g:** flap-glide only, with wings increasingly flexed in the glide phase as speed rises.

### Gaps
- I could not get the exact flapping-percentage-vs-speed table or the beats-per-burst numbers from Tobalske et al. 1999.
- I found no data for starling flap-gliding duty cycles or woodpecker bounding numbers (Tobalske 1996), and no field data for large flap-gliders (e.g., ibis, Portugal et al. 2014) in accessible text.

## 6. Gliding and soaring: posture vs glide speed

### Takeaway
Gliding birds fold their wings progressively as glide speed rises, so span and area shrink together while chord stays constant. In the jackdaw, wingspan falls linearly from almost fully spread at about 6 m/s to about 70% of maximum at 11 m/s. The tail is fanned at low speed and fully furled above about 9 m/s, and the feet are lowered as air brakes at the slowest speeds. Swifts sweep their wings back at high glide speeds and extend them for slow glides and turns. An empirical rule fits a jackdaw, a Harris' hawk and a laggar falcon: span ratio = 1 at stall speed and about 0.75 at twice stall speed.

### Cited Findings
**Jackdaw (Corvus monedula, 0.184 kg, maximum span 0.569–0.595 m, wing area 0.059 m², aspect ratio about 5.6–6.0; tilted wind tunnel; Rosén & Hedenström 2001)**
- **Speeds.** It glided steadily at **6–11 m/s**, with minimum-sink speed **7.4 m/s** (sink **0.62 m/s**) and best-glide speed **8.3 m/s**. Peak lift-to-drag ratio was **12.6 at 8.5 m/s** (glide ratio about 13.4:1). The bird glided most comfortably at 8.5–10 m/s. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001, JEB 204:1153](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Glide polar.** Sink speed (m/s) = 0.0424V² − 0.624V + 2.916, with V the airspeed in m/s. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Wingspan vs speed.** Span **b (m) = −0.028V + 0.705** over 6–11 m/s (r² = 0.79). Span ratio is **−0.0488V + 1.239**, about 0.95 at 6 m/s and about 0.70 at 11 m/s. The wing was close to fully stretched only at the lowest speeds. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Area vs span.** Area ratio ≈ span ratio (slope 0.97), and mean chord was constant at about 0.095 m regardless of speed. The wing folds along a single geometric path, with S = 0.0665b² + 0.0316b + 0.0156. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Tail.**
  - Tail area **decreased linearly from 6 to 9 m/s** (Stail = −0.0016V + 0.0203 m²) and was **fully furled at ≥ 9–9.5 m/s**.
  - Maximum spread at 6.1 m/s was **2.1×** the furled area.
  - [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Feet.**
  - Feet were lowered at ≤ 8 m/s.
  - At 6 m/s the feet were down at about 60° with toes spread for drag.
  - At 6.5 m/s the tarsi were at about 45°, and at 7 m/s the toes were closed.
  - At ≥ 8.5 m/s the feet were fully retracted.
  - [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Lift coefficient.** CL was 1.66 at 6 m/s. Estimated CL,max is about 2.1, giving a stall speed of about 4.9 m/s. Primaries splay at slow speed. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **General span-ratio rule.** **β = 5/4 − V/(4·Vmin)**, with **Vmin = √(2mg / (ρ·S·CL,max))**. β is 1 at Vmin and about 3/4 at 2·Vmin, bounded at a minimum of about 1/3. This fits the jackdaw, Harris' hawk (Vmin 6.1 m/s) and laggar falcon (Vmin 6.6 m/s), with r² = 0.79–0.98. A pigeon reaches a minimum span ratio near 1/3 (Pennycuick 1968). The black vulture showed no span reduction with speed. A default CL,max of 1.8 is suggested for unknown species. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Tunnel behaviour.** Even in the tilted tunnel, the jackdaw cycled: it drifted back, then **accelerated forward with a series of wingbeats** before resuming the glide. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Later jackdaw work.** Wingspan and wing area both fall with speed and are roughly proportional, and tail span converges to about 7.5% of maximum wingspan at high speed. — [KleinHeerenbrink & Hedenström, JEB 219:1572](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/219/10/1572/14647/Wake-analysis-of-aerodynamic-components-for-the) (search snippet; which of the two follow-up papers this comes from is unconfirmed)
- **Swifts** (Apus apus, swift wings measured in a wind tunnel).
  - **Extended wings are superior for slow glides and turns, and swept wings for fast glides and turns.**
  - Choosing the right sweep can **halve sink speed or triple turning rate**.
  - Swept wings make less lift in high-speed turns but can bear the extreme loads.
  - [Lentink et al. 2007, Nature 446:1082](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature05733)
- **Why sweep helps.** The main benefit of the swept-wing posture in high-speed glides is **drag reduction**, not enhanced lift. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Cost of gliding.** Gliding costs an estimated 3–4× basal metabolic rate, for isometric muscle work holding the wings out. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)
- **Long soaring flights.** Andean condors soared for up to more than 5 h and about 172 km without flapping. — [sci.news, Williams et al. 2020](https://www.sci.news/biology/andean-condor-flight-08638.html)

### Inferences
- For animation, drive glide wing extension from airspeed: full span with fanned tail, splayed primaries and possibly lowered feet near stall (about 1.2× Vmin). Fold linearly to about 75% span at 2× Vmin and lower beyond, adding wrist flexion and backward sweep of the hand. The tail furls once the bird is above about 1.5–1.8× Vmin.
- Thermal circling, which is slow and needs maximum lift and tight turns, should use the fully extended, tail-fanned posture. Fast inter-thermal or slope glides should use the swept, tail-furled posture. This is extrapolated from the jackdaw and swift data, not measured directly.

### Gaps
- I found no source with quantitative thermal-vs-slope soaring posture data (dihedral angles, sweep angles in degrees). The Lentink et al. sweep angles in degrees and Henningsson & Hedenström swift gliding data were not accessible.
- I found no data on dihedral changes with speed.

## 7. Hovering and slow flight

### Takeaway
Hovering and very slow flight sit on the expensive left side of the power curve. Apart from hummingbirds, birds support weight only on the downstroke in hovering and slow flight, using a tip-reversal or strongly flexed upstroke, near-vertical body and high amplitude. Hummingbirds get about 25% of weight support from the upstroke. Small passerines flap almost continuously near 0 m/s (about 99% of the time).

### Cited Findings
- **Who can hover.** Hummingbirds are the only birds that can sustain hovering in still air. In rufous hummingbirds about **75% of weight support comes from the downstroke and 25% from the upstroke**. In all other species studied, only the downstroke supports weight during hovering and slow flight. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight), citing Warrick et al. 2005
- **Slow-flight upstroke.** Pointed-winged birds (pigeon) use a **tip-reversal upstroke at slow speed** (illustrated at 6 m/s). — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Zebra finch near 0 m/s.** Flapping share is about 99% at 0 m/s. — [Tobalske et al. 2005](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/208/15/2895/15765/Contractile-activity-of-the-pectoralis-in-the)
- **Power and size.** Power is higher at hover than at intermediate speeds; examples include cockatiel about 140 W/kg at 0 m/s against about 75 W/kg at minimum (figure reading). Larger hummingbirds use proportionally larger wings and **greater wingbeat amplitudes**, which lowers induced costs. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Pigeons in slow flight.**
  - The tail is broadly spread at low speeds, including takeoff and landing.
  - Pressure measurements suggest more aerodynamic power is produced at distal wing regions when birds fly slowly while accelerating or decelerating (Usherwood et al. 2005).
  - [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Turning in slow flight.** Pigeons and two parrot species initiate rolls during the downstroke. Pigeons start a roll with higher downstroke velocity on the outer wing and stop it with higher velocity on the inner wing in late downstroke. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)

### Inferences
- A non-hummingbird "hover" or very slow flight should use a near-vertical body (about 60–80°), a stroke plane near horizontal relative to the world, large amplitude (wingtips nearly touching above and below), a tip-reversal or strongly flexed upstroke with no lift on the upstroke, a fanned tail and higher WBF than cruise. Most birds sustain this only for a few beats.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative WBF or amplitude values for pigeon or passerine hovering, and no hummingbird WBF numbers in accessed sources.

## 8. Landing: deceleration, pitch-up, flare, final beats

### Takeaway
Most deceleration happens in the air before touchdown. Pigeons slow from about 4.2 m/s to about 1 m/s over the final 4 beats, and both doves and zebra finches touch down at about 1 m/s. The bird pitches its whole body up (body angle reaching about 72° and sometimes over 90°), which rotates the stroke plane to tilt upward (+17°), pushing air forward to brake. The wings move forward (more protraction), the tail rotates near-vertical while staying broadly spread, and WBF and amplitude rise again up to the second-to-last beat before dropping on the final beat. Peak leg forces on landing are about half those of takeoff.

### Cited Findings
**Pigeon, wingbeats counted back from foot-down (−3, −2, −1, 0; Berg & Biewener 2010)**
- **Velocity.** Horizontal velocity fell from **4.19 ± 0.27 m/s (start of beat −3) to 0.97 ± 0.32 m/s (end of beat 0)**. By the final upstroke-downstroke transition, the birds were at a quarter of their pre-landing speed: "most of the deceleration occurred during flight". — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Deceleration.** Deceleration grew with each beat, peaking at **−8.50 ± 2.52 m/s² in beat 0**. Landing velocity varied more between flights than takeoff velocity did. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Body pitch-up.** Body angle at mid-downstroke rose to **72.0 ± 7.8° in beat 0**, against 19° in mid-flight, and occasionally exceeded 90°. Body angle rose by 85° in total from takeoff beat 1 to landing beat 0. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Stroke plane.** The local stroke plane angle went from **−0.9 ± 1.7° (beat −3) to +17.3 ± 7.4° (beat 0)**, tilting upward so the downstroke is directed forward. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Wing planes.** The arm wing, proximal hand and distal hand plane angles all **increased >90° from takeoff to landing**, so the wings face more vertically and act as air brakes. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Protraction.** Wing protraction increased from **6.2 ± 2.0 cm (beat −3) to 10.7 ± 1.3 cm (beat 0)**, against 2.7 ± 2.5 cm in mid-flight, placing the wingstroke further forward of the shoulder. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Frequency and amplitude.**
  - WBF rose from mid-flight to a peak of **7.78 ± 0.25 Hz at beat −1**, then dropped to **6.81 ± 0.67 Hz on beat 0**.
  - Sweep angle rose from 176° (mid-flight) to **192 ± 7° at beat −1**, then fell to **174 ± 10° at beat 0**.
  - Downstroke sweep velocity peaked at 2.45 °/ms at beat −1 and was 2.11 °/ms at beat 0, lower than in takeoff.
  - [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Tail.** Tail angle reached **78.3 ± 13.7° at beat −1**, close to vertical, while spread stayed at about 150° on average. Spread was **not** significantly greater in landing. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Perch familiarity.** Pigeons approach faster as they become familiar with a perch (Green & Cheng 1998). Starlings exert less force on the perch when landing than when taking off (Bonser & Rayner 1996). — cited in [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)

**Zebra finch and diamond dove (Provini et al. 2014)**
- Both species landed at about **1 m/s**, but differed in when their last wingbeat came relative to touchdown. — [PubMed 24855670](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/24855670)
- Wing aerodynamic forces were **greater in the final phase of landing than in the initial phase of take-off**. The wing-to-leg force ratio was higher in doves than in finches, and **peak landing ground-reaction forces were about half of takeoff forces**, which the authors read as active control of landing. — [Provini et al. 2014, JEB 217:2659](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/15/2659/12207/Transition-from-wing-to-leg-forces-during-landing); [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/17241355/Transition_from_wing_to_leg_forces_during_landing_in_birds)

**Gliding approach (jackdaw)**
- At glide speeds close to stall (about 6 m/s), the jackdaw had its tail fully fanned, its wings near full span and its feet lowered at about 60° with toes spread for drag. — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)

### Inferences
- A landing sequence for a pigeon-sized bird over the final 3–4 beats:
  - body pitch ramps from about 20° to about 70–90° (flare);
  - the stroke plane rotates with the body to point the downstroke forward;
  - the wing reach moves forward;
  - WBF and amplitude rise about 15–20% above cruise;
  - the final beat is weaker and lower in frequency;
  - the tail is near-vertical and fanned;
  - legs extend for touchdown at about 1 m/s.
  - Peak deceleration is about 0.9 g on the final beat.

### Gaps
- I found no numbers for landing on the ground versus a perch, landings with height changes (potential and kinetic energy were held constant in the pigeon study), or large soaring birds' landings.

## 9. Acceleration rates and how flap frequency and amplitude relate to acceleration

### Takeaway
In the best wingbeat-resolved dataset (pigeons), wingbeat frequency, amplitude and downstroke angular velocity are highest when horizontal acceleration is highest (takeoff) and fall beat by beat as acceleration falls. They rise again for deceleration, but braking relies more on body and stroke-plane rotation than on downstroke velocity. The direction of force (accelerating vs braking) is set mainly by pitching the whole body, because the stroke plane stays at about −48° relative to the body throughout. Peak horizontal accelerations are about ±9 m/s² (about ±0.9 g) in pigeons.

### Cited Findings
- **Peak values.** Horizontal acceleration peaks at **+9.14 ± 2.22 m/s²** (takeoff beat 2) and **−8.50 ± 2.52 m/s²** (landing beat 0) in pigeons. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Power-related variables.** Downstroke amplitude, WBF and downstroke velocity "were all greatest during takeoff flight and decreased with each successive takeoff wingbeat". **Beat 1 values are 8.88 Hz, 203° and 3.16 °/ms; mid-flight values are 6.57 Hz, 176° and 2.32 °/ms.** — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing). The mid-flight sweep velocity of about 2.3 °/ms is read from Fig. 4C, so it is approximate.
- **Stroke plane fixed to the body.** The local stroke plane angle is almost entirely set by body angle: **SPA_loc = 0.99 × body angle − 48.0 (R² = 0.95)**. The stroke plane relative to the body averaged **−48.2 ± 7.0°** across takeoff, mid-flight and landing. Wing plane angles (R² 0.93–0.96) and tail angle (R² 0.78) also follow body angle. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Pitch control is cheap.** The estimated moment arm needed to pitch the body between phases is only **1.4 mm** (takeoff to mid-flight) and **1.7 mm** (mid-flight to landing), using a pigeon pitch moment of inertia of 0.00144 kg·m² and 4.2 N body weight. Very subtle kinematic changes are therefore enough to switch between accelerating and decelerating modes. — [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)
- **Magpies in steady tunnel flight.** Magpies changed upstroke posture with acceleration and deceleration. Magpies and pigeons alternated steady flight with acceleration and deceleration even at fixed tunnel speeds, especially at intermediate speeds. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight); [Tobalske & Dial 1996](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/199/2/263/7465/Flight-Kinematics-of-Black-Billed-Magpies-and)
- **Amplitude vs frequency (secondary summary).** Amplitude, not WBF, increases with climb rate and acceleration in pigeons. — [Krishnan et al. 2022 preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.28.497935v1.full) (secondary; unverified)
- **Muscle power.** Takeoff and acceleration muscle power is about 400 W/kg, against 60–150 W/kg in cruise. — [Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)
- **Gliders.** Gliding birds re-accelerate with a short series of wingbeats (jackdaw in a tilted tunnel). — [Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)

### Inferences
- A practical mapping for animating velocity and acceleration, with a as forward acceleration in g:
  - **WBF factor** ≈ 1 + about 0.35·(a / 0.9 g) when accelerating (8.88 vs 6.57 Hz). When braking near landing, about 1 + 0.18 on the second-to-last beat, then back toward cruise.
  - **Amplitude factor** ≈ 1 + about 0.15·(a / 0.9 g) when accelerating (203 vs 176°), and about 1.09 near peak braking.
  - **Body pitch**: from about −13° at maximum forward acceleration, through about +19° in cruise, to +70–90° at maximum braking.
  - **Stroke plane** stays fixed at about −48° relative to the body.
  - **Wing protraction** is small in cruise and large while braking.
  - At steady speeds, keep WBF near cruise and let speed show through body pitch, stroke plane and upstroke posture.
- These are pigeon-derived and roughly linearised from four-beat sequences. Small passerines will instead modulate flapping duty cycle (bursts vs bounds), and large soarers will flap almost only during takeoff and acceleration.

### Gaps
- I found no study regressing WBF or amplitude directly against instantaneous acceleration across many species, and no data on acceleration in level flight (speeding up mid-air, as opposed to takeoff).
- Hedrick et al. 2004 (cockatiel wing inertia and whole-body acceleration within the wingbeat) and Askew et al. 2001 (quail takeoff power) were not accessible in full.
