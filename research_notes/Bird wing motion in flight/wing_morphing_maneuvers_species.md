# Bird Wing Morphing, Maneuvering Postures, and Species Differences (focus: swallows, swifts, starlings)

Scope note: These notes cover how birds change wing, tail, and body shape to turn, bank, pitch, dive, brake, and handle gusts, and how wing shape and flight style differ by species. Several key primary papers (Ros et al. 2011 PNAS, Lentink et al. 2007 Nature, Shelton et al. 2014 JEB, Hedrick et al. 2007 JEB) were paywalled or only partly readable. Where a number comes from an abstract, a press release, or a search-engine summary rather than the full paper, this is flagged.

## 1. Turning and banking: how do birds start and carry out a turn?

### Takeaway
Across the species studied, birds turn mainly the way aircraft and helicopters do: they roll (bank) the whole body and point their roughly fixed, body-aligned lift vector into the turn. Roll is started by left-right asymmetries in the wingstroke. In cockatoos these include wing-area reduction on one side and differences in arc and angle of attack; in pigeons they are subtle changes in wing trajectory. In cliff swallows, high-speed turns are roll-based and mostly done while flapping, not gliding.

### Cited Findings
**Pigeons (Columba livia), slow 90° level turns (Ros et al. 2011 PNAS)**
- Ros, Bassman, Badger, Pierson & Biewener (2011, PNAS 108(50)) filmed pigeons (mean body mass ~319 g, n = 3) doing level 90° turns in a hallway. They concluded that pigeons "steer like helicopters," using whole-body rotations to change the direction of aerodynamic force — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PNAS..10819990R); [Biewener lab](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/pigeon-turning.md)
- "The net aerodynamic force averaged over the downstroke was maintained in a fixed direction relative to the body throughout the turn." Early in the turn the body reorients (mainly by rolling/banking into the turn) to redirect the downstroke force and change the flight path. Later the bird mostly rolls back to its straight-flight orientation without much further change in path — [Phys.org, 2011](https://phys.org/news/2011-11-pigeons-bodies-wing.html)
- The pigeons did not flap faster or harder on the outside wing. They "simply turn or roll" the body while the wings keep a near-normal flapping pattern — [Phys.org, 2011](https://phys.org/news/2011-11-pigeons-bodies-wing.html)
- During these slow turns the upstroke produced about 50% of the downstroke force, "nearly matching" hummingbirds — [Phys.org, 2011](https://phys.org/news/2011-11-pigeons-bodies-wing.html)
- Follow-up (Ros et al. 2015 JEB, "Pigeons produce aerodynamic torques through changes in wing trajectory during low speed aerial turns"): the 90° turns did not appear to be driven by contralateral asymmetries in wing speed, wing area, angle of attack, wingbeat amplitude, or timing. Torques came from changes in wing trajectory. (This is from a search summary of the abstract, not the full text.) — [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40VnJOP9)

**Rose-breasted cockatoo (Eolophus roseicapillus), low-speed turns (Hedrick, Usherwood & Biewener 2007, JEB 210:1897 and 1912)**
- The turns were built from banked flapping. Roll was linked to asymmetric biceps activation at the start and end of each turn, which led to the hypothesis that birds turn via left-right asymmetry in wing area (one wing partly flexed) — [Biewener lab pub. I](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/low-speed-maneuvering-flight-rose-breasted-cockatoo-eolophus-roseicapillus-i); [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/475612663.pdf)
- After the first wingbeat of the turn, the cockatoos kept rolling at decreasing angular velocity until mid-turn, reaching a maximum roll (bank) angle of about 70° — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/475612663.pdf)
- JEB's summary ("Cornering cockatoos"): at the start of the turn, the outside wing swept a larger arc and rotated at the shoulder to meet the air at a steeper angle than the inside wing. Near the end of the turn, the inside wing swept the larger arc, which reversed the roll to level out — [JEB Inside summary](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/210/11/i/16817/CORNERING-COCKATOOS)
- Part II concluded that maneuvers rely on asymmetries spread across the whole flight apparatus rather than on dedicated "turning muscles." Bird roll damping coefficients (~ -1.5) were 2–6 times those of typical aircraft (~ -0.45), so birds must keep driving a roll and stop rotating quickly once the asymmetry ends — [Biewener lab pub. II](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/low-speed-maneuvering-flight-rose-breasted-cockatoo-eolophus-roseicapillus-ii); [SICB abstract](https://sicb.org/?p=32425)

**Cliff swallows (Petrochelidon pyrrhonota), high-speed chases (Shelton, Jackson & Hedrick 2014, JEB 217:2717)**
- High-speed turns are roll-based. Turns mostly involved active flapping rather than gliding. Centripetal force in typical maneuvers peaked at ~2 body weights and varied only slightly with flight speed — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/475610907.pdf); [Phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2014-07-cliff-swallows-flight-tactics-missile.pdf)
- In tandem (chase) flights, the follower matched the leader's path and wingbeat frequency (~12.3 Hz) while staying slightly above it. The leader turned away from the follower's side ~65% of the time. Speeds ranged from 1.0 to 15.6 m/s and chases lasted 0.72–4.71 s — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/475610907.pdf)
- In barn and cliff swallow pursuits, the follower's accelerations, angular accelerations, and angular velocities tracked the leader's with phase lags of ~89 ms, 71 ms, and 87 ms — [SICB abstract](https://sicb.org/abstracts/high-speed-pursuit-in-barn-and-cliff-swallows/)

**Starling flocks (modelled and empirical)**
- In the StarDisplay model (Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt), birds have simplified aerodynamics (lift, drag, gravity). To fly a curve they roll into the turn until they reach a bank angle, as an aircraft does — [Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2011, PLoS ONE (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3150374)
- Because birds roll into turns and do not change speed much, a flat, wide flock becomes long and narrow after a 90° turn — [University of Groningen news, 2011](https://www.rug.nl/news/2011/08/113_11hemelrijk); [PMC3150374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3150374)
- Later StarDisplay versions reportedly use asymmetric banking controls tuned from starling video: banking control 10 for inner neighbours vs 1 for outer. (This comes from a search summary; I did not confirm which paper states it.) — [Storms et al. 2019, Behav Ecol Sociobiol](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00265-019-2734-4)
- During zig-zag escape waves in starling flocks, waves weaken most likely because birds reduce their maximum bank angle, not because they copy neighbours less — [Damping of waves of agitation in starling flocks](https://openpolar.no/Record/ftunigroningenpu:oai:pure.rug.nl:publications%2F08e96e64-5f47-4171-8e85-021218ebb350)
- In real starling flocks (Attanasi et al. 2014, Nature Physics), a turn starts with a few birds, often at the flock edge, and spreads through the flock at a roughly constant speed with negligible attenuation ("superfluid"-like). Propagation speed increases with the flock's orientational order — [Springer / Nature Physics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nphys3035); [arXiv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1303.7097)

**Turning with asymmetric wing/finger morphing (robot evidence)**
- On PigeonBot (real pigeon feathers, 4 servo-driven wrist/finger joints controlling 40 elastically linked feathers), either asymmetric wrist motion or asymmetric finger motion alone was enough to start a turn. This suggests birds may steer partly with the hand wing — [search summary of Chang et al. 2020, Science Robotics; TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/01/16/pigeonbot-brings-flying-robots-closer-to-real-birds); [Iowa State course copy](https://www.engineering.iastate.edu/~shermanp/AERE355/AERE331/Morphing%20Wings%20and%20Underactuated%20Feathers%201_17_20.pdf)

### Inferences
- For animation, the dominant visual cue of a turn is body roll toward the inside of the turn, with the wings still beating more or less symmetrically relative to the body. The lift vector stays roughly perpendicular to the wing plane and tilts with the body. A bird that turns without rolling would look wrong.
- Roll entry should be brief and asymmetric: for a wingbeat or two, the inside wing is partly flexed or swept (smaller area), and the outside wing takes a bigger or steeper stroke. Roll exit is the mirror image. Because roll damping is high, a bird stops rolling almost as soon as the asymmetry stops; there is little "coasting" roll.
- Bank angles of ~70° are documented for slow cockatoo turns. Swallows pulling ~2 g in routine turns imply bank angles around 60° in a level turn (cos⁻¹(1/2) = 60°), and steeper banks for harder pulls. This is my calculation from the load-factor value, not a measured bank angle.
- Swallows turn while flapping, not gliding. Starlings in flocks bank into turns at near-constant speed, so a flock turn looks like a wave of rolling birds sweeping across the group.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Ros et al. 2011 full text (PDF unreadable, PNAS/Harvard pages blocked), so I have no pigeon bank angle, turn radius, or speed values.
- I found no measured bank angles or roll rates (deg/s) for swallows, swifts, or individual starlings in free flight.
- I found no published turn radii for swallows or swifts in the material reached.

## 2. Pitch control, diving, and braking

### Takeaway
Birds control pitch and speed mainly by moving the wings fore-aft (sweep) and by folding them (elbow/wrist), with help from the tail. Diving raptors move through a sequence of shapes: fully tucked "teardrop," then "cupped," then "M-shape." In the M-shape the hand wings sweep out to make vortex lift for the pull-out. At low speed (braking, slow flight), birds raise body angle, angle of attack, and tail spread.

### Cited Findings
**Peregrine falcon stoop and pull-out (Ponitz et al. framework; Selim & Gowree review)**
- **Teardrop:** wings fully retracted against the body, tail furled. Used for maximum speed early in the stoop, with little control authority — [Gowree/Selim, "The Peregrine Falcon's Dive" (arXiv 2008.03948)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)
- **Cupped:** wing trailing edges stay attached to the tail while the leading edges sweep outward to form an air "cup." Used for attitude correction and slight braking. Lateral wing forces can reach ~3× body weight during attitude adjustment — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)
- **Open-cupped:** a transitional shape between cupped and M-shape, used for control — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)
- **M-shape (pull-out):** the inner wing is forward-swept and the hand wing sweeps between about 40° and 90°. The trailing edge separates from the body/tail. Deploying the hand wing creates extra vortex lift, like a delta wing. Aspect ratio shifts from ~0.95 to ~4.9, which allows much higher lift coefficients without a large drag penalty — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)
- In the pull-out the tail stays furled and the wings act like elevons on a tailless delta. Roll and yaw come from asymmetric in-plane and out-of-plane wing morphing (including bank turns). Side force in the cupped shape gives yaw control. Peregrines have no fin or rudder — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)
- The forward-swept part of the wing pushes outer-wing flow inboard, and stoop aerodynamics are vortex-dominated. Attack success depends largely on maneuverability in the second (pull-out) phase, as the bird switches between cupped and other silhouettes — [Gowree et al. 2018, Communications Biology](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-018-0029-3)
- Speeds: field-experiment equilibrium speed was 22.5 m/s, and peregrines can reach about 80 m/s — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948). Another source cites 39–51 m/s dive speeds — [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/falcons-high-speed-dive-generates-forces-needed-to-catch-agile-prey/)
- High stoop speed maximizes the aerodynamic forces available for maneuvering against erratic prey (simulation study; Mills et al. 2018, PLoS Comp Biol) — [Audubon](https://audubon.org/news/research-reveals-exactly-why-peregrine-falcons-are-so-deadly); [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/falcons-high-speed-dive-generates-forces-needed-to-catch-agile-prey/)

**Pitch trim by elbow/wrist folding (gulls)**
- In hybrid glaucous-winged × western gulls, folding the wrist raised trim (glide) speed in >80% of configurations, and folding the elbow did so in 60%. Reducing forward sweep raised trim speed and steepened the glide angle in every configuration tested. Trim speeds spanned 11.8–29.8 m/s — [Harvey et al. 2022, PNAS (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410)
- Joint-driven wing morphing (elbow/wrist) effectively controls lift, pitching moment, and static margin. Other mechanisms (e.g., tail, shoulder sweep) are still needed to trim — [Harvey et al. 2021, J R Soc Interface](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1402698306729525248); [UBC summary](https://biology.zoology.ubc.ca/article/new-publication-harvey-et-al-journal-royal-society-interface-see-abstract)
- Hawks and pigeons counter pitch disturbances with fore-aft wing movement, often with tail input (no numbers given) — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)

**Slow flight / braking posture (barn swallow)**
- In barn swallows (Hirundo rustica) flown at 4–14 m/s in a wind tunnel, body tilt, tail spread, and angle of attack all increased as speed fell. The authors interpret this as the tail adding lift at low speed — [Park, Rosén & Hedenström 2001, JEB (Stirling repository)](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/306); [core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/9048411.pdf)

**Perching pitch-up (steppe eagle)**
- Steppe eagles (Aquila nipalensis) morph their wings into a distinct M-shape during the pitch-up phase of perching (Carruthers, Thomas & Taylor 2007) — [MDPI Aerospace alula review](https://www2.mdpi.com/2226-4310/8/10/295)

### Inferences
- Postures for animation:
  - **Fast dive:** wings fully folded to the body and tail closed (teardrop).
  - **Controlled dive:** wings partly out with wrists forward, cupped, tail closed.
  - **Pull-out or hard braking:** the wing forms an "M." Wrists stay forward, hand wings swing out and back, and the tail fans and lowers.
  - **Slow flight or landing:** body pitched nose-up, tail spread wide, wings at a high angle of attack.
- Speeding up in a glide reads visually as folding the wrist (hand wing swept back, shorter span). Slowing down reads as extending the wing and spreading the tail.

### Gaps
- I found no direct measurements of g-loads in real peregrine pull-outs. The values (3.2–3.5 g measured; ~10 g predicted) are from model and field estimates in a review that is internally inconsistent (see Q7).
- I found no quantitative data on swallow or starling diving or braking postures beyond the barn swallow wind-tunnel trends.

## 3. Wing morphing: sweep, span, area, dihedral, and their effect on lift, drag, and turning

### Takeaway
Birds morph wings mainly through the shoulder (sweep, dihedral) and the coupled elbow-wrist (folding, which reduces span and area). Feathers slide passively over one another as the skeleton moves. In swifts, the right sweep can roughly halve sink speed or triple turn rate. Extended wings are best for slow glides and turns, and swept wings for fast glides and turns. In gulls, the elbow and wrist trim glide speed and can switch the bird between stable and unstable pitch configurations.

### Cited Findings
**Swifts (Apus apus), Lentink et al. 2007, Nature 446:1082–1085**
- Wind-tunnel tests on real swift wings, combined with a glide model: the right sweep can roughly halve sink speed or triple turning rate. "Extended wings are superior for slow glides and turns; swept wings are superior for fast glides and turns." — [Lund University record (abstract)](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/168570); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v446y2007i7139d10.1038_nature05733.html)
- Swept wings produce less lift in fast turns but withstand high loads. Cost-effective gliding occurs at ~8–10 m/s, and agility figures of merit peak at ~15–25 m/s — [Lund University record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/168570)
- Swifts roost in flight at night at 8–10 m/s with wings outstretched. Swept-back wings tolerate the higher centripetal acceleration of high-speed turns at up to 30 m/s — [Physics Today, 2007](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/morphing-wings-help-birds-control-gliding)
- Shape and size change come from varying feather overlap, driven by an articulated skeleton and muscles, during both flapping and gliding — [Physics Today, 2007](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/morphing-wings-help-birds-control-gliding)
- Press coverage (secondary): extended wings carry swifts 1.5× farther and keep them aloft 2× longer in slow, straight flight — [Nature news, 2007](https://www.nature.com/news/2007/070423/full/news070423-7.html)

**Gulls (Larus glaucescens × L. occidentalis), Harvey et al. 2022 PNAS / 2021 J R Soc Interface**
- Modelled joint ranges were elbow 86°–164°, wrist 106°–178°, shoulder sweep −20° to +20°, and shoulder dihedral 10°–20°. Of these configurations, 1,457 trimmed, with trim speeds of 11.8–29.8 m/s; the shallowest trim glide angle was −12.2° — [Harvey et al. 2022 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410)
- With 0° dihedral and 0° sweep the gull wing could not trim (negative zero-lift pitching moment). Increasing dihedral and sweeping the wings forward raised the zero-lift pitching moment and let more configurations trim — [PMC9477410](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410)
- 1,331 of the trimmed configurations were statically stable. The 126 unstable ones had extended wrists. The authors hypothesize gulls extend the wrist to become unstable for "a more sensitive reaction to control inputs" while maneuvering (not tested directly). Heavy phugoid damping implies an otherwise "sluggish response" — [PMC9477410](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410)
- Short-period frequency ranged 8.9–41.0 rad/s and phugoid 0.45–1.10 rad/s — [PMC9477410](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410)
- In Harvey's thesis, lower wrist angles were both statically and dynamically stable. Elbow and wrist act as adaptive controls that switch the bird between stable and unstable flight — [Harvey thesis, U. Michigan](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/handle/2027.42/174351)

**Pigeons (Columba livia), elbow-wrist coupling (Stowers, Matloff & Lentink 2017, J R Soc Interface)**
- Elbow and wrist motion are coupled in 3D, not by the planar "drawing parallels" linkage proposed in 1839. Wrist bones, especially a sliding ulnare, contribute to morphing (micro-CT over 7 consecutive poses) — [search summary; figshare supplementary](https://rs.figshare.com/collections/Supplementary_material_from_How_pigeons_couple_three-dimensional_elbow_and_wrist_motion_to_morph_their_wings_/3832504)
- Feather angles move linearly with wrist angle during morphing. Elastic ligaments link each feather to the skeleton, so all flight feathers follow wrist and finger motion automatically (the feather system is "underactuated") — [Iowa State course copy of Stanford material](https://www.engineering.iastate.edu/~shermanp/AERE355/AERE331/Morphing%20Wings%20and%20Underactuated%20Feathers%201_17_20.pdf); [SICB abstract](https://sicb.org/?p=8305)

**Peregrine falcon:** in the M-shape, hand-wing sweep varies ~40°–90° and aspect ratio ~0.95–4.9 across stoop shapes (see Q2) — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)

### Inferences
- Morphing in a 3D model can be driven by one or two "fold" parameters: elbow and wrist flex together, and the primaries fan or stack in proportion to wrist angle. This matches the pigeon finding that feathers follow the skeleton linearly.
- Speed mapping for swift- and swallow-like birds: slow, lazy circling uses fully extended, near-straight wings (low sweep, maximum span). Fast hawking dashes and tight high-speed turns use swept-back, sickle-shaped wings with reduced span.
- Gull-type gliders should show more dihedral (wings raised above the body) in slow, stable glides, and folded, swept wrists in fast glides.

### Gaps
- I could not get the actual sweep angles, span/area reductions, or turn-rate values (deg/s) from Lentink et al. 2007; the full Nature text is paywalled.
- I found no direct wing-morphing measurements for swallows or starlings.
- I found no data on anhedral use in maneuvering birds.

## 4. Alula, tail spread/fanning, and feather overlap

### Takeaway
The alula (thumb feathers) is deployed at slow speed and high angle of attack, as in landing, perching, and sharp low-speed maneuvers. It is thought to delay stall, either through a small tip vortex or by keeping a leading-edge vortex on the hand wing; the exact mechanism is debated. The tail adds substantial lift at low speed and in gliding raptors, and it is spread more as speed drops. In swallows, the outer tail streamers appear to aid tight turns. Overlapping flight feathers are held together by hooked microstructures that act as directional fasteners, so the wing stays a continuous surface while it morphs.

### Cited Findings
**Alula**
- Lee, Kim, Park, Jabłoński & Choi (2015, Scientific Reports 5:9914; not PNAS) used magpie wings. They attributed the alula's stall-delaying effect to a small vortex formed at the tip of the alula feathers, with a streamwise vortex aft of the alula and a stall-delaying effect outboard of it — [MDPI Aerospace review](https://www2.mdpi.com/2226-4310/8/10/295); [Phys.org 2015](https://phys.org/news/2015-06-small-vortex-wing-elegance-birds.pdf)
- Later work questioned whether that vortex is an alula tip vortex rather than the alula's separated boundary layer. A review argues the alula's main role is to induce or maintain a leading-edge vortex over the hand wing, raising lift during slow-speed maneuvering. The mechanism "remains debated" — [MDPI Aerospace review](https://www2.mdpi.com/2226-4310/8/10/295)

**Tail**
- Thomas (1993) proposed treating a spread bird tail as a low-aspect-ratio delta wing — [summary in search results citing swallow tail literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1691798)
- Barn swallows spread the tail more as flight speed falls, along with increased body tilt and angle of attack. The tail adds lift at low speed — [Park et al. 2001 (Stirling)](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/306)
- Usherwood et al. 2020 (JEB) tracked soap bubbles behind a gliding barn owl, tawny owl, and goshawk. Downwash behind the body/tail consistently exceeded that behind the wings, meaning the tail carries high lift. Raptors appear to accept lower passive pitch stability to reduce total (viscous) drag — [Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/471784/); [RVC news](https://rvc.ac.uk/research/research-centres-and-facilities/structure-and-motion/news/birds-in-flight-share-lessons-for-future-aircraft-designs)
- Barn owls deflect the tail downward for lift (tail:body length ~0.38). In peregrines the tail acts as a trailing-edge flap (tail:body ~0.35) but stays furled during the pull-out. Faster birds such as swifts and hawks need larger tails for yaw stability (no numbers given) — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)
- In a starling (Sturnus vulgaris), the furled tail appears to work as a splitter plate that reduces parasite drag — [search result summary of starling/tail literature](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/4795294/624235.pdf)

**Swallow tail streamers**
- A 1994 study (author not shown in the snippet I saw; widely attributed to R.Å. Norberg) used high-speed film of foraging barn swallows plus wind-tunnel work. It proposed that the streamers make the tail's leading edge deflect downward when the tail is spread, which delays flow separation and allows tighter turns — [Deakin repository, Buchanan 2001 review on hirundine streamers](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/The_function_and_evolution_of_the_tail_streamer_in_hirundines/20845885)
- Experimental manipulation of streamer length in barn swallows, compared with sand martins (no streamers), indicates that the barn swallow streamer plays a role in maneuvering flight — [HKU Hub](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/230712)
- In house martins, maneuverability measures predicted optimal performance with outer tail feathers 6–10 mm longer than natural. Longer feathers lowered mean velocity and acceleration — [Matyjasiak et al. (scite listing)](https://scite.ai/authors/jolanta-matyjasiak-eDwZP2)
- Whether streamers evolved mainly for maneuverability (natural selection) or through female choice (sexual selection) is still debated, and both likely contributed — [Oxford Behavioral Ecology PDF](https://academic.oup.com/beheco/article-pdf/12/2/157/9742063/120157.pdf)

**Feather overlap and fasteners**
- Matloff et al. 2020 (Science 367:293–297): hook-shaped microstructures on neighbouring flight feathers form a directional fastener ("directional Velcro") that stops feathers from separating as the wing extends. Elastic connective tissue passively redistributes the overlapping feathers as the skeleton moves — [EurekAlert news release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/513703); [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/barbs-of-a-feather-lock-together)
- The fasteners occur across bird species except silent fliers such as owls; Physics Today reports 12 of 15 other species surveyed. On PigeonBot the fasteners unlocked easily so the wings could flex to a tucked position for turns — [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/barbs-of-a-feather-lock-together); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/513703)

### Inferences
- Animation: the tail should fan wide in slow flight, braking, landing, and tight low-speed turns, and fold to a narrow wedge in fast cruise and dives. A swallow's forked tail opens into a broad triangle during sharp turns and slow hawking, and closes into a narrow fork with streamers trailing during fast straight dashes.
- The tail probably twists or tilts toward the inside of a turn to help yaw/roll. This is an inference from the "no fin/rudder, wing-tip and tail control" framing; I found no measured tail-twist angles.
- The alula, a small feather tuft at the wrist's leading edge, pops up only at high angle of attack (landing, flaring, slow sharp turns), not in cruise.
- As a wing folds, primaries should slide under one another as a stacked fan, with no gaps opening between feathers.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative tail-spread angles (degrees) versus speed or maneuver for swallows, swifts, or starlings.
- I found no measurements of tail twist or tilt during turns.
- The author of the 1994 streamer study was not confirmed from a fetched source.

## 5. Gust and turbulence response

### Takeaway
Birds reject gusts partly through passive mechanics: the wings hinge freely at the shoulder and act like a vehicle suspension. In a gliding barn owl hit by vertical gusts, the wings swung up at the shoulder and the body and head stayed almost steady, cutting the impulse transmitted to the torso by ~32% in the first ~80 ms before active or aerodynamic responses took over. Gulls can also tune their pitch-gust response by changing elbow and wrist angles.

### Cited Findings
- Cheney, Usherwood et al. (2020, Proc R Soc B) filmed a barn owl ("Lily") gliding through fan-generated vertical gusts. Her wings rose rapidly about the shoulder, and inertial effects reduced the predicted impulse to the torso and head by 32% over the first 80 ms, before aerodynamic mechanisms took over. The fastest part of the response is built into wing mechanics and needs no active control — [ScienceDaily, 2020](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/10/201021085117.htm); [RVC news](https://www.rvc.ac.uk/research/research-centres-and-facilities/structure-and-motion/news/lily-the-barn-owl-reveals-how-birds-fly-in-gusty-winds?page=109); [RVC repository](https://rvc-repository.worktribe.com/output/1375074)
- Gust strength is reported inconsistently. The RVC/ScienceDaily coverage says the strongest gust was as fast as the owl's flight speed, while Stevenson et al. 2023 describe the owl's gusts as 40–70% of flight speed, wider than the wingspan — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/10/201021085117.htm); contrasted with [Stevenson et al. 2023, R Soc Open Sci (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10170351)
- The owl's response was an "immediate rotation of its wings around the shoulder joints." In the owl, the centre of pressure and centre of percussion lie within 0.067 wing-lengths of each other, which is the condition for the body to be shielded from the gust impulse — [Stevenson et al. 2023 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10170351)
- Bird wings have roughly linear spanwise mass distributions, which puts the centre of percussion near mid-wing. Elliptical lift puts the centre of pressure at 0.42 span. Many bird wings stall "softly" at their Reynolds numbers, with no abrupt loss of lift — [Stevenson et al. 2023 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10170351)
- In a bird-scale hinged-wing aircraft model (8.0 m/s flight, 2.4 m/s peak upward gust), the fuselage barely reacted for ~100 ms with the heaviest wing. A constant hinge torque gave the cleanest rejection, and stiffer hinges reduced the benefit — [Stevenson et al. 2023 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10170351)
- Gulls: with elbow at 130° and sweep at 15°, stable configurations halved a pitch disturbance in 0.05–0.12 s (short period) and 2.22–2.52 s (phugoid). Unstable (wrist-extended) configurations doubled in 0.03–0.04 s (short period) and 0.80–1.69 s (phugoid). By changing wrist and elbow, gulls can adjust how they respond to perturbations — [Harvey et al. 2022 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410); [Futurity](https://www.futurity.org/gulls-flight-wing-shape-2794752-2/)

### Inferences
- In turbulence, a bird's head and body should stay steady while the wings flick up (or down) at the shoulder and settle back within about a tenth of a second. Wingtips can "flutter" around a stable body. A good procedural rule: perturb wing elevation at the shoulder, not body pitch or height.
- Gliding birds in gusty air (gulls, swifts in glide) show small, rapid wrist and elbow adjustments rather than whole-body bobbing.

### Gaps
- I did not find the original Cheney et al. 2020 shoulder-elevation angles in degrees.
- I found no gust-response data for swallows, swifts, or starlings.

## 6. Species differences: wing shape categories and flight styles (swallows, swifts, starlings, pigeons, gulls, finches, raptors)

### Takeaway
Wing shape follows the classic Savile scheme: elliptical (short, rounded), high-speed (pointed, swept, high loading), high-aspect-ratio (long, slender), and slotted high-lift (broad with spread primaries). Flight style tracks shape. Swifts flap-glide with long scimitar wings and roost on the wing. Swallows (hirundines) hunt with agile flapping and short glides and turn in tight flapping rolls. Starlings and other small passerines alternate flapping with flap-gliding at slow speed and flap-bounding (wings folded) at higher speed. Pigeons turn slowly by whole-body roll. Gulls glide with variable wrist and elbow fold. Falcons stoop with tucked and M-shaped wings.

### Cited Findings
**Wing-shape categories**
- Savile's four wing types, still widely used:
  - **Elliptical:** short, rounded; tight maneuvering in dense cover (Accipiter hawks, many passerines, especially non-migratory ones).
  - **High-speed:** flat, pointed, shortish, heavy wing loading (peregrine, most ducks).
  - **High-aspect-ratio:** long, slender, for efficient soaring; aspect ratio ~8 in gulls, up to ~15 in albatrosses.
  - **Slotted high-lift / soaring:** broad wings with slotted primaries for low-speed soaring and takeoff in confined spaces (eagles, vultures, many raptors).
  — [Wikipedia: Bird wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_wing); [Royal BC Museum wing types sheet](https://learning.royalbcmuseum.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Bird-Wing-Types2-1.pdf)
- The two controlling metrics are aspect ratio (shape) and wing loading (body weight per wing area). Savile classified wings by shape and flight behaviour, not structure, and the categories overlap — [Wikipedia: Bird wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_wing)

**Flight-style categories from radar (Bruderer et al.)**
- Tracking-radar and cine data for 153 W. Palaearctic species sorted flight into three styles. Continuous flapping is used by waders and ducks. Irregular flapping and gliding (flap-gliding) is the swift pattern. Regular flapping and bounding (flap-bounding) is the songbird/passerine pattern — [PNNL Tethys record](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/wing-beat-characteristics-birds-recorded-tracking-radar-cine-camera)

**Swifts (Apus apus)**
- Swifts flap-glide (Bruderer radar class above) — [PNNL Tethys](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/wing-beat-characteristics-birds-recorded-tracking-radar-cine-camera)
- They roost on the wing at 8–10 m/s with wings extended and switch to swept wings for fast flight and high-speed turns. Agility peaks around 15–25 m/s — [Lund record of Lentink et al. 2007](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/168570); [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/morphing-wings-help-birds-control-gliding)
- Gliding swifts reach a maximum lift-to-drag ratio of 12.5 at 9.5 m/s (tilted wind tunnel, Henningsson & Hedenström 2011). An earlier flapping-flight L/D of ~13 was later corrected; the claim that swifts beat other birds in effective L/D is "no longer supported" — [search summary of Henningsson & Hedenström JEB papers; Lund record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/4414358)
- Flapping swifts had higher span efficiency (more efficient lift production) than gliding swifts. Flapping costs more mainly because of added wing profile drag. The swift wing appears tuned for lift production in flapping rather than for gliding alone — [Henningsson et al. 2014, PLoS ONE (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/6deca58ae5ad4863affde56681afc35b)

**Swallows and martins (Hirundinidae)**
- Barn swallow wind-tunnel kinematics (4–14 m/s): wingbeat frequency vs speed is U-shaped, with minima at ~8.7–8.9 m/s. The birds insert upstroke pauses (wings briefly held, i.e., flap-gliding) at medium and high speeds to adjust force output, like flap-bounding in other species — [Park et al. 2001 (Lund record)](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/296b9425-556f-4c3b-97da-f7c0b11f0998); [Stirling](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/306)
- Cliff swallows are considered highly maneuverable because their body and wing shapes suit rapid direction changes. They chase at 1.0–15.6 m/s with ~12.3 Hz wingbeats, make roll-based flapping turns at ~2 g peak, and reach up to ~8 g in competitive or prey-capture maneuvers (Hedrick, quoted in press) — [core.ac.uk (Shelton et al. 2014)](https://core.ac.uk/download/475610907.pdf); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2014-07-cliff-swallows-flight-tactics-missile.pdf)
- Barn swallow tail streamers aid tight maneuvering (see Q4) — [HKU Hub](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/230712)

**Starlings (Sturnus vulgaris)**
- A wind-tunnel starling at 12 m/s cruise had a wingbeat frequency of ~13.3 Hz and a peak-to-peak wingtip vertical amplitude of ~28 cm — [arXiv 1306.0146, near-wake of a freely flying starling](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.0146)
- Starlings (like budgerigars) use both intermittent flight modes: flap-gliding at lower speeds and flap-bounding (wings folded against the body between flapping bursts) at higher speeds — [Tobalske 2007 review (scilit)](https://www.scilit.net/publications/29035c3d384193f9d934326f9e5c095c); [University of Montana](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/biomechanics-of-bird-flight/)
- Murmurations: birds bank into turns at near-constant speed, and turns spread from a few initiators across the flock (see Q1) — [PMC3150374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3150374); [Nature Physics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nphys3035)

**Finches and small passerines generally**
- Small passerines typically flap-bound: they alternate flapping bursts with bounds where the wings are folded flat against the body (Tobalske & Dial 1994). Bounding seems limited to birds below roughly 0.3 kg — [Tobalske 2007 (scilit)](https://www.scilit.net/publications/29035c3d384193f9d934326f9e5c095c); [PMC2390671 (review excerpt)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2390671)

**Pigeons, gulls, raptors:** see Q1–Q3. Pigeons turn by whole-body roll with near-symmetric strokes. Gulls trim glide speed by elbow and wrist fold (trim 11.8–29.8 m/s). Peregrines use teardrop, cupped, and M-shape stoop configurations. Goshawks and owls get high lift from the tail in glides — [PMC9477410](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410); [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948); [Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/471784/)

### Inferences
- **Swallow look:** long, pointed, swept wings; deeply forked tail (streamers in barn swallows). Flight alternates quick flapping bursts with short glides and brief wing-held pauses. The bird rolls steeply while flapping through turns, and the tail fans open in tight turns and at low speed.
- **Swift look:** very long, narrow, sickle-shaped wings with a short arm and long hand; short forked tail. Flight is fast, stiff, shallow wingbeats alternating with long glides. Wings are extended straight out in slow cruise or roosting and swept back like a crescent in fast dashes and tight high-speed turns.
- **Starling look:** pointed, near-triangular wings of moderate length; short, square tail. Flight is rapid flapping with glides, then wings folded briefly in bounds at speed. In flocks the birds bank together at near-constant speed.
- **Finch look:** an undulating, bounding flight path. Wings snap shut against the body during each bound.

### Gaps
- I found no specific aspect ratio or wing loading values for barn swallows, common swifts, or starlings in the material reached. Henningsson & Hedenström and Rayner/Norberg tables are the likely sources but were not accessible.
- I did not verify a Cornell Lab of Ornithology / Handbook of Bird Biology description of swallow flight style.
- I could not confirm the "triangular wing" description of starlings from a primary source; it is a field-guide-style descriptor used here as an inference.

## 7. Quantitative data: roll rates, turn rates, g-forces

### Takeaway
Reliable numbers are sparse:
- Cliff swallows: ~2 g peak in typical roll-based turns, with up to ~8 g quoted for extreme chases.
- Cockatoos: up to ~70° bank in slow turns, with roll damping 2–6× that of aircraft.
- Swifts: the right sweep can triple turn rate, and swept wings tolerate high-speed turns up to ~30 m/s.
- Peregrines: estimated at ~3–3.5 g in field pull-outs and up to ~10 g predicted.
I found no direct roll-rate (deg/s) measurements for small birds.

### Cited Findings
- Cliff swallow centripetal force peaks at ~2 body weights in typical high-speed, roll-based turns, nearly independent of speed — [core.ac.uk (Shelton et al. 2014)](https://core.ac.uk/download/475610907.pdf)
- Maximum turning performance in competitive actions and prey capture is ~8 g (Hedrick, quoted in press). This is a press quote and is not verified in the paper — [Phys.org (via search summary)](https://phys.org/news/2014-07-cliff-swallows-flight-tactics-missile.pdf)
- Rose-breasted cockatoo: maximum bank ~70° at mid-turn. Roll angular velocity decreases after the first wingbeat of the turn. Roll damping coefficient is ~ -1.5 vs ~ -0.45 for aircraft — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/475612663.pdf); [Biewener lab pub. II](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/low-speed-maneuvering-flight-rose-breasted-cockatoo-eolophus-roseicapillus-ii)
- Swift: sweep choice can triple turning rate. Swept wings handle high-speed turns up to 30 m/s — [Lund record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/168570); [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/morphing-wings-help-birds-control-gliding)
- Peregrine pull-out: field estimate of ~3.2 g in one section and ~3.5 g in the conclusion of the same review (internally inconsistent). Loads may roughly triple in wild stoops, and up to almost 10 g is predicted in high-speed pull-outs. Up to ~47 N lift at 80 m/s at the tightest part of the pull-out. Lateral forces up to ~3× body weight in the cupped shape — [arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)
- Pigeon: slow 90° level turns, with upstroke force ~50% of downstroke force — [Phys.org 2011](https://phys.org/news/2011-11-pigeons-bodies-wing.html)
- Swallow chase reaction lags: ~71–89 ms between leader and follower angular and linear accelerations — [SICB abstract](https://sicb.org/abstracts/high-speed-pursuit-in-barn-and-cliff-swallows/)
- Wingbeat frequencies:
  - Cliff swallow ~12.3 Hz in chases — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/475610907.pdf)
  - Starling ~13.3 Hz at 12 m/s — [arXiv 1306.0146](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.0146)
  - Barn swallow minimum wingbeat frequency near 8.7–8.9 m/s — [Lund record](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/296b9425-556f-4c3b-97da-f7c0b11f0998)

### Inferences
- In a level turn, load factor n = 1/cos(bank), so ~2 g corresponds to a ~60° bank, and 8 g to ~83°, nearly knife-edge. This is my calculation, not a reported measurement.
- At swallow speeds of ~10 m/s, a 2 g level turn gives centripetal acceleration of about √(2² − 1) × 9.81 ≈ 17 m/s², so the radius is r = v²/a ≈ 100/17 ≈ 6 m. Again, this is a derived estimate, not a measured radius.
- High roll damping means roll should be animated as quick roll-in, hold, quick roll-out, with no overshoot or residual wobble.

### Gaps
- I found no published roll rates (deg/s) or yaw rates for swallows, swifts, starlings, or pigeons in accessible sources.
- I found no measured turn radii for any of the focal species.
- The ~8 g swallow figure is from a press quote I could not check against the paper.
- I found no starling individual g-load or bank-angle measurements; the flock models use bank angle as a parameter but I did not find calibrated values.
