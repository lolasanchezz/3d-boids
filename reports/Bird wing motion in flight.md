# Folding wings and banked bodies sell flight

A real wingbeat is not the symmetric sine that most boids demos use: birds drive the wing down fast and fully spread, then spend the longer part of the cycle (about 55–68% of it in house martins and pigeons) on an upstroke in which the elbow and wrist fold the wing, cutting span to 30–40% in swallow-like birds, before the joints unfold in a shoulder-to-tip ripple that makes the wingtip whip at the bottom of the stroke. Across flight phases, the most useful pattern for an animator is that birds keep wingbeat tempo almost constant in steady flight and show speed through posture instead: body pitch, the tilt of the stroke plane, how much the upstroke folds, and how often they pause to glide or bound. Tempo and amplitude jump only in unsteady flight. A pigeon's first takeoff beat is 35% faster and about 15% wider than cruise with the body pointing nose-down, and its last landing beats tilt the body up to about 72° so the same stroke becomes an air brake. Gliding birds shrink their span roughly in proportion to speed and close the tail, and turning birds roll the whole body while both wings keep beating almost alike, stopping the roll almost as soon as they stop driving it. In a boids simulation all of this fits in a small per-boid rig of about ten parameters: a wingbeat phase warped so the downstroke takes about 40% of the cycle, a flap curve plus an upstroke-only fold pulse, flapping effort driven by acceleration and climb rather than raw speed, a flap/glide/bound/pause state machine, a speed-dependent glide span, and a bank angle taken from sideways acceleration. For this repo's swallow sprites, upstroke folding and banking are worth the most, because they are what change a silhouette seen from below; the limit on all of this is that most numbers come from pigeons and wind-tunnel birds, and nobody has measured roll rates or bank limits for small flocking birds.

## Each wingbeat pairs a fast, spread downstroke with a slower, folded upstroke

The shoulder does the flapping and the elbow and wrist do the shaping. X-ray motion capture (XROMM) of chukars in steep climbing flight shows the humerus (upper arm bone) raising and lowering through **about 102°**, swinging forward and back through 45° and twisting about its own axis through 36°, while the **elbow folds through about 90° (from 127° open to 35° closed) and the wrist through about 90° (from 148° to 58°)** ([Baier, Gatesy & Dial 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)). The timing of those two joints creates the shape change. The elbow is fully open at 46% of the downstroke, so the wing is at full span through mid-downstroke; it is most folded about 45% of the way into the upstroke, while the wrist stays open until roughly 40% into the upstroke, so the wing is narrowest in mid-to-late upstroke ([Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)). These chukar values come from climbing at about 60° and 2 m/s, so treat them as an upper bound for level cruising. In pigeons the elbow and wrist are mechanically linked through a six-bar linkage that includes a sliding wrist bone, the ulnare ([Stowers, Matloff & Lentink 2017](https://www.peeref.com/works/4113142)), which means a single "fold" parameter can drive both joints and still be faithful to the anatomy.

The downstroke is the shorter half of the cycle, which runs against the common animation instinct to linger on the power stroke. In house martins (*Delichon urbica*, a swallow relative) in a wind tunnel, the downstroke takes **only 32–41% of the cycle**, and its absolute length stays near **35 ms at every speed** while the upstroke stretches from about 60 to 80 ms ([Rosén, Spedding & Hedenström 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)). Pigeons in slow flight show the same bias, with a downstroke of about 67 ms and an upstroke of about 83 ms at 6.67 Hz ([Spedding, Rayner & Pennycuick 1984](https://typeset.io/pdf/momentum-and-energy-in-the-wake-of-a-pigeon-columba-livia-in-4nt38b6vbe.pdf), abstract only). The flight muscles explain the rhythm. The pectoralis, which powers the downstroke, hits peak force in the first half of the downstroke, so the wing accelerates hardest early ([Robertson & Biewener 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-f736-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content), abstract only). The smaller supracoracoideus peaks not in mid-upstroke but at the turn from downstroke to upstroke, braking the wing at the bottom and flinging it back up, and its power output matches the cost of moving the wing's mass on the upstroke ([Tobalske & Biewener 2008](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/contractile-properties-pigeon-supracoracoideus-during-different-modes-flight)). That mass is expensive to move: a pigeon optimal-control model needs over 34 W at 6 m/s, but only 8.7 W if wing mass is ignored ([Parslew 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4277085)), which is why folding the wing on the way up pays. The reversal at the bottom also ripples outward. The elbow reverses about 4% of a cycle before the wingtip and the wrist about 2% before it, whereas at the top the joints and tip turn almost together ([Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)). On screen, that means a tip that whips at the bottom of the stroke and turns over more softly at the top.

How much the wing folds depends on wing shape and speed, and the upstroke is the part that varies: "downstroke kinematics are relatively invariant, upstroke kinematics differ among species and according to flight speed" ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)). Pointed-winged budgerigars, cockatiels and doves use a **tip-reversal upstroke at or below 7–9 m/s**, in which the hand rotates leading-edge-up and flicks up and back, and switch to a **swept-wing upstroke** above that speed; round-winged magpies use a flexed, feathered upstroke at every speed ([Tobalske, Hedrick & Biewener 2003](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/wing-kinematics-of-avian-flight-across-speeds/)). Pigeons pass through all three styles, from tip-reversal at 6 m/s to feathered at 10 m/s to swept at 16 m/s ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)). At the extremes, hummingbird wings stay nearly rigid with a span ratio of about **0.93** ([Tobalske et al. 2007](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/three-dimensional-kinematics-of-hummingbird-flight/)), while house martins pull their wingtips in close to the body and pause there in mid-upstroke, with span ratios of **0.31–0.41** ([Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)). The upstroke does some work, but not much: tip-reversing cockatiels get about 14% of downstroke force from it ([Hedrick, Usherwood & Biewener 2004](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/207/10/1689/1548750/1689.pdf), abstract only), and in house martins at 4 m/s it contributes almost nothing to holding the bird up, though its share grows with speed ([Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)). The sources disagree about which way span ratio moves with speed in general. The house martin's falls from 0.41 to 0.31 between 6 and 10 m/s, and its authors call that unusual, implying other species open the upstroke wider as they fly faster ([Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)); Tobalske's 2007 review, by contrast, is summarized as saying span ratio generally *decreases* with speed ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)). For a swallow flock the conflict does not change the choice: the house martin is the closest relative measured.

Twist, feathers and the wingtip's path complete the picture. The humerus tilts leading-edge-down through the downstroke and starts rotating back about 29% of the way through the wingbeat, midway through the downstroke ([Baier et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655074)), and a spinning pigeon wing posed in tip-reversal positions meets the air at angles of attack of about **46° at mid-downstroke and 66° at mid-upstroke** ([Crandell & Tobalske 2011](https://typeset.io/pdf/aerodynamics-of-tip-reversal-upstroke-in-a-revolving-pigeon-3d13rw0x24.pdf), abstract only). On the downstroke the primary feathers overlap into a closed surface; on the upstroke they rotate and open into slots. Tiny hooks on neighbouring feathers act like one-way Velcro that keeps the surface closed as the wing extends ([Matloff et al. 2020, via EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/513703)), and the feathers follow the bones passively: Stanford's PigeonBot moves 40 real feathers with only four wrist and finger motors ([TechCrunch on Chang et al. 2020](https://techcrunch.com/2020/01/16/pigeonbot-brings-flying-robots-closer-to-real-birds)). Seen from the side relative to the shoulder, a pigeon's wingtip draws a loop in cruise but a **figure-of-eight in takeoff, landing and slow flight** ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). A hummingbird's hovering figure-of-eight opens into an ellipse in forward flight ([Tobalske et al. 2007](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/210/13/2368/1539805/2368.pdf), abstract only), and the house martin's tip path needs a second component at twice the wingbeat frequency, growing with speed, to capture its mid-upstroke pause ([Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)).

## Cruising birds hold their tempo and let posture carry speed

Body size sets the baseline tempo. Pennycuick fitted **f = 1.08 · m^(1/3) · g^(1/2) · b^(−1) · S^(−1/4) · ρ^(−1/3)** to field data from 32 species, where m is mass, g gravity, b wingspan, S wing area and ρ air density ([Pennycuick 1990](https://next.sciencegate.app/document/10.1242/jeb.150.1.171)). For birds of the same shape at different sizes this reduces to f ∝ m^(−1/6), and accelerometers carried by albatrosses and petrels match it almost exactly for cruising (exponent −0.18) but find a steeper −0.30 for takeoff, which is limited by maximum muscle power ([Sato et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2670537)). A second rule links tempo to speed. Flying and swimming animals cruise at a **Strouhal number of 0.2–0.4** (frequency × peak-to-peak tip travel ÷ speed), the range where flapping propulsion is most efficient ([Taylor, Nudds & Thomas 2003](https://www.biology.ox.ac.uk/node/1030831)), and birds sit at the low end. Nudds and colleagues explained 90% of the variation in wingbeat frequency across 60 species using a Strouhal number of 0.21 for continuous flappers and 0.25 for intermittent ones, with a stroke angle of **θ ≈ 67 · b^(−0.24) degrees** ([Nudds, Taylor & Thomas 2004](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/oxford-uuid:7ebcc4ec-c34a-413c-b83d-6a60d3e8d0ef), abstract only). That fit gives about 93° for a 0.25 m span, about 90° for a 0.3 m swallow-sized span, 74° for a pigeon and 51° for an albatross: small birds flap through wider arcs.

Within one species, steady-speed tempo barely changes. Magpies at 4–14 m/s and pigeons at 6–20 m/s showed **no appreciable change in wingbeat frequency** while their body angle fell as speed rose ([Tobalske & Dial 1996](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/199/2/263/7465/Flight-Kinematics-of-Black-Billed-Magpies-and)). Barn swallows trace only a shallow U, lowest near **8.7–8.9 m/s** ([Park, Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-pdf/204/15/2741/1238033/2741.pdf)); house martins slow by about 14% from 4 to 10 m/s ([Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)); and starlings are reported, from a search snippet only, to flap faster roughly in proportion to airspeed ([JEB](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/2853086/jexbio_187_1_1.pdf)). Wild bar-headed geese show why birds avoid changing tempo: their frequency varied by only about 0.6 beats per second within a flight, yet **a 5% rise in frequency came with an estimated 41% rise in flight power** ([Bishop et al. 2015, via EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/570597)). Birds instead adjust power by recruiting more or fewer pectoralis muscle fibres ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)), and the visible changes are postural. In budgerigars, cockatiels, doves and magpies the **stroke plane becomes more vertical and the wing sweeps farther as speed rises** (Tobalske, Hedrick & Biewener 2003, cited in [Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)), the body levels out, and the upstroke changes style. Muscle power against speed is U-shaped, with cockatiels falling from about 140 W/kg when hovering to about 75 W/kg at 5–7 m/s and climbing to about 230 W/kg at 13 m/s, but **the speed with the lowest wingbeat frequency need not be the speed with the lowest power** ([Tobalske et al. 2003](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/comparative-power-curves-bird-flight)).

Many birds also vary how often they flap. Flap-gliding (wings held out between bursts) saves the most power at slow to moderate speeds, while flap-bounding (wings folded against the body between bursts) saves power at high speeds, helped by lift from the body ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)). Size draws the lines. **Birds over about 300 g, like pigeons, glide but never bound**, and pigeons fold their wings progressively more during glides as speed rises; **small birds under 30 g with rounded wings bound almost exclusively**; species that do both are under 300 g or pointed-winged ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight); [Tobalske & Dial 1996](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/199/2/263/7465/Flight-Kinematics-of-Black-Billed-Magpies-and)). Budgerigars show the speed shift cleanly, spending about 80% of their non-flapping time gliding at 7 m/s but about 55% in full bounds at 13–16 m/s ([Tobalske 2007, Fig. 5](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)). Zebra finches flap-bound at every speed, with the flapping share falling as speed rises ([Tobalske, Peacock & Dial 1999](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/202/13/1725/7978/Kinematics-of-flap-bounding-flight-in-the-zebra)) from about 99% when hovering to about 63% at 8 m/s ([Tobalske et al. 2005](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/208/15/2895/15765/Contractile-activity-of-the-pectoralis-in-the), abstract only). The flocking birds relevant here spread across these modes. Barn swallows insert **brief pauses in the upstroke** at medium and high speeds ([Park et al. 2001](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-pdf/204/15/2741/1238033/2741.pdf)) and house martins hold their wingtips close to the body in a mid-upstroke pause ([Rosén et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373391)); tracking radar classes swifts as irregular flap-gliders and passerines as regular flap-bounders ([Bruderer et al., via Tethys](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/wing-beat-characteristics-birds-recorded-tracking-radar-cine-camera)); starlings glide at low speed and bound at higher speed ([Tobalske 2001](https://bioone.org/journals/american-zoologist/volume-41/issue-2/0003-1569(2001)041[0177:MVAIFI]2.0.CO;2/Morphology-Velocity-and-Intermittent-Flight-in-Birds1/10.1668/0003-1569(2001)041[0177:MVAIFI]2.0.CO;2.full)); and at the far end, Andean condors flap only about 1% of their flight time, spending more than 75% of that flapping on takeoff ([Williams et al. 2020](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020PNAS..11717884W)).

## Takeoff and landing reuse one wingbeat across an 85° pitch swing

Takeoff is a leg jump followed by the hardest wingbeats of the flight. When a pigeon's feet leave the perch, early in the first downstroke, it already has **74% of the speed it will have by the next turn of the stroke**, and starlings and quail have 80–90% ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). Estimates of the legs' share depend on how it is measured, from about a quarter of the acceleration over four beats in pigeons to about 95% over roughly three beats in zebra finches and doves ([Provini et al. 2014](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/15/2659/12207/Transition-from-wing-to-leg-forces-during-landing), abstract only), but every study agrees the legs launch the bird. The wings then take over with their largest, fastest strokes. Wingbeat frequency starts at **8.88 Hz on beat 1, about 35% above the 6.57 Hz of mid-flight**, and falls to 7.68 Hz by beat 4; the downstroke sweeps 203° in the first two beats against 176° in cruise, often with the wingtips touching at the top and bottom; and downstroke angular speed falls from 3.16 to 2.51 °/ms over four beats ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). Forward acceleration peaks on **beat 2 at about 9.1 m/s² (about 0.9 g)**, and the bird is still speeding up at beat 4 (from 1.52 to 4.51 m/s), so reaching steady flight takes more than four beats. The body tips **nose-down to about −13°** at mid-downstroke on beat 1 (cruise is +19°) and the stroke plane tilts to −61°, pushing air backward for thrust ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). Muscle output reaches about **400 W/kg, against 60–150 W/kg in cruise**, and round-winged gamebirds that fold the wing on the level-flight upstroke switch to a tip-reversal upstroke for takeoff ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)).

Landing runs the same machinery in reverse, with most of the braking done in the air. Over the last four wingbeats a pigeon's forward speed drops from **4.19 to 0.97 m/s**, with deceleration growing each beat to a peak of **−8.5 m/s² on the final beat** ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). The body tilts up to **about 72° at mid-downstroke on the final beat**, sometimes past 90°; the stroke plane rotates from about −1° to **+17°**, so the downstroke now pushes forward; and the arm and hand surfaces rotate more than 90° between takeoff and landing, turning the wings into air brakes. The wings also reach farther forward of the shoulder (10.7 cm on the final beat against 2.7 cm in cruise), tempo and sweep rise again to **7.78 Hz and 192° on the second-to-last beat** before dropping to 6.81 Hz and 174° on the last, and the tail tilts nearly vertical (about 78°) while staying fanned ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). Doves and zebra finches touch down at about 1 m/s, with peak landing forces about half those of takeoff ([Provini et al. 2014](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/217/15/2659/12207/Transition-from-wing-to-leg-forces-during-landing)). A gliding approach looks different again: a jackdaw gliding near stall fans its tail fully, holds its wings near full span, and lowers its feet at about 60° with toes spread as extra drag ([Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)).

The bird does not invent a new stroke for each phase. Across takeoff, cruise and landing, the pigeon's stroke plane stays locked at about **−48° relative to the body** (stroke-plane angle = 0.99 × body angle − 48.0, R² = 0.95), and the angles of the wing surfaces and tail also follow body angle ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). The switch from accelerating to braking comes mainly from tilting the body through about 85°, and moving between the two modes needs a shift in balance point of only about **1.4–1.7 mm**. Climbing and descending follow the same logic: a secondary summary reports that pigeon wingbeat frequency does not differ significantly between climbing, level and descending flight, while amplitude rises with climb rate and acceleration ([Krishnan et al. 2022](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.28.497935v1.full); numbers not verified), and slow descents use the same forward-reaching wing posture seen at the end of landing ([Berg & Biewener 2010](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/10/1651/9685/Wing-and-body-kinematics-of-takeoff-and-landing)). Speeding up in mid-air, as opposed to taking off, has barely been studied. Magpies change their upstroke posture as they speed up or slow down, and magpies and pigeons both alternate bursts of speeding up and slowing down even at a fixed wind-tunnel speed ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)), a steady wobble around cruise speed that is itself worth animating.

## Gliding birds shrink and sweep their wings as they speed up

Gliding is wing reshaping driven by airspeed. A jackdaw in a tilted wind tunnel glided steadily at 6–11 m/s, and its **span fell steadily from about 95% of maximum at 6 m/s to about 70% at 11 m/s** (b = −0.028V + 0.705 m), with wing area falling in step and average chord holding at about 9.5 cm, so the wing folds along a single path ([Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)). Its tail was fanned to 2.1 times its closed area at 6 m/s and **fully closed above about 9–9.5 m/s**; its feet hung at about 60° with toes spread as air brakes at 6 m/s and were tucked away above 8.5 m/s; its primaries spread apart at the slow end; and its best glide ratio, about 12.6, came at 8.5 m/s. The authors generalized this into a rule that fits the jackdaw, a Harris' hawk and a laggar falcon: span ratio **β = 5/4 − V/(4·Vmin)**, which equals 1 at stall speed, falls to about 3/4 at twice stall speed and bottoms out near 1/3 (a value pigeons reach), with Vmin = √(2mg/(ρ·S·CL,max)) and a default maximum lift coefficient of 1.8 for species where it is unknown. Black vultures are an exception and do not fold. Even in steady tunnel glides the jackdaw cycled, drifting back and then speeding up with a short burst of wingbeats before gliding again ([Rosén & Hedenström 2001](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/2545997/624228.pdf)).

Swifts show why sweeping the wings back matters. Tests on real swift wings found that choosing the right sweep can **halve sink speed or triple turning rate**, with extended wings winning in slow glides and turns and swept wings in fast ones ([Lentink et al. 2007](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/168570)); swifts sleep on the wing at 8–10 m/s with wings outstretched ([Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/morphing-wings-help-birds-control-gliding)), and sweeping back mainly cuts drag rather than adding lift ([Tobalske 2007](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/210/18/3135/17027/Biomechanics-of-bird-flight)). Gulls use the same joints to set glide speed. In a model of gull wings, folding the wrist raised steady glide speed in over 80% of wing shapes tested and folding the elbow did so in 60%, across glide speeds of 11.8–29.8 m/s, and only wrist-extended shapes were unstable in pitch, which the authors suggest gulls use to respond faster when maneuvering ([Harvey et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9477410)). Diving peregrines move through a fully tucked "teardrop" for top speed, a "cupped" shape with leading edges swept out for attitude control, and an "M-shape" pull-out in which the hand wings swing out for extra lift, raising aspect ratio from about 0.95 to 4.9 ([Selim & Gowree, arXiv 2008.03948](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.03948)); steppe eagles use a similar M-shape when pitching up to perch ([MDPI alula review](https://www2.mdpi.com/2226-4310/8/10/295)).

Two smaller appendages and one automatic reflex finish the gliding look. The tail carries real lift at low speed: barn swallows spread it wider, tilt the body more and raise angle of attack as speed falls ([Park et al. 2001](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/306)), soap-bubble tracking behind gliding owls and a goshawk shows more downwash behind the tail than behind the wings ([Usherwood et al. 2020](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/471784/)), and experiments that changed tail length indicate barn swallows' streamers help in tight turns ([HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/230712)). The alula, a small feather tuft on the wrist's leading edge, pops up at slow speed and high angle of attack, as in landing, though how it delays stall is still debated ([MDPI review](https://www2.mdpi.com/2226-4310/8/10/295)). When a gust hit a gliding barn owl, its wings swung up at the shoulder while its head and body stayed nearly level, cutting the jolt reaching the body by **32% in the first 80 ms** before any active control ([Cheney et al. 2020, via ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/10/201021085117.htm)); this works because the wing's centre of pressure nearly coincides with its centre of percussion, the point where a sudden push produces no jolt at the shoulder ([Stevenson et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10170351)). Turbulence on a real bird therefore looks like wings flicking around a steady body, not a body bobbing up and down.

## Turning is a whole-body roll, and flock turns travel as rolling waves

Birds turn the way aircraft and helicopters do, rolling the body so their lift points into the turn. Pigeons making slow 90° turns keep the average downstroke force **fixed relative to the body throughout the turn** ([Ros et al. 2011](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011PNAS..10819990R)); they do not flap faster or harder with the outside wing but simply roll while the wings keep beating normally, and during those turns the upstroke produced about half as much force as the downstroke ([Phys.org, 2011](https://phys.org/news/2011-11-pigeons-bodies-wing.html)). The small torques that start the roll come from slight changes in the wings' paths ([Ros et al. 2015](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40VnJOP9), abstract only). Rose-breasted cockatoos show where the two wings do differ: at turn entry the outside wing sweeps a bigger arc and meets the air at a steeper angle, and at exit the inside wing takes the bigger arc to level the bird out ([JEB summary](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/210/11/i/16817/CORNERING-COCKATOOS)), with bank peaking around **70°** mid-turn ([Hedrick et al. 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/475612663.pdf)). Air resists a bird's roll **2–6 times as strongly as an aircraft's** (damping coefficient about −1.5 against −0.45), so a bird must keep driving a roll and stops rotating almost as soon as it stops pushing ([Biewener lab](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/low-speed-maneuvering-flight-rose-breasted-cockatoo-eolophus-roseicapillus-ii)). On PigeonBot, an uneven wrist or finger motion alone was enough to start a turn ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/01/16/pigeonbot-brings-flying-robots-closer-to-real-birds)).

Swallows turn hard while flapping. Cliff swallows chasing each other at 1.0–15.6 m/s make **roll-based turns, mostly while flapping rather than gliding**, with peak turning force of about **2 times body weight** ([Shelton, Jackson & Hedrick 2014](https://core.ac.uk/download/475610907.pdf)); up to about 8 g is quoted in press coverage but has not been checked against the paper ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2014-07-cliff-swallows-flight-tactics-missile.pdf)). By my calculation, not a measurement, a level 2 g turn implies about a 60° bank (load = 1/cos(bank)) and, at 10 m/s, a radius of about 6 m. In barn and cliff swallow chases, the follower's accelerations and turning rates track the leader's with delays of **71–89 ms** ([SICB abstract](https://sicb.org/abstracts/high-speed-pursuit-in-barn-and-cliff-swallows/)), a usable per-boid reaction delay.

Flocks add a pattern that plays out across many birds. Real starlings, and the StarDisplay model built on them, roll into turns instead of changing speed: their speed varies by only about **1–1.5%** (coefficient of variation, against 5–20% in fish schools), banking costs lift so a turning flock briefly loses height, and rolling into and out of a turn run at separate rates with a pull back toward level ([Hemelrijk & Hildenbrandt 2011](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0022479)). Turns spread through starling flocks as an almost undamped wave at **20–40 m/s** while the birds fly at 7–12 m/s, so a 400-bird flock can be swept by a turn in a little over half a second ([Attanasi et al. 2014](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1303.7097)). The dark "agitation waves" that ripple through murmurations are modelled as zig-zag escape moves passed between neighbours, and they fade mainly because birds reduce their maximum bank angle, not because they copy neighbours less ([Storms et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00265-019-2734-4)). Since the darkening a viewer sees comes from wings turning face-on or edge-on as birds bank, per-bird roll is a signal visible at flock scale, not just a per-bird detail (an inference from the model, not a direct measurement). Reynolds' 1987 boids paper already called for this, banking each bird so its "up" axis lines up with gravity plus centripetal acceleration, which pilots call a coordinated turn ([Reynolds 1987](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~dt/siggraph97-course/cwr87/)).

Wingbeat timing between birds does not lock in dense flocks. Pigeons in tight flocks flap *faster* than alone, by about 0.1 Hz near neighbours, with no aerodynamic advantage; the cost is put down to the work of control and collision avoidance ([Usherwood et al. 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10164); [Sci. Nat. 2019](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00114-019-1641-x)). Random wingbeat timing per bird is therefore the right default for murmurations. Ordered V-formations differ: northern bald ibises in diagonal V slots flap **in spatial phase** with the bird ahead, so the follower's wingtip retraces the leader's path through the air, while birds directly behind another flap close to the opposite phase to avoid its downwash ([Portugal et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12939)). Because the leader's wake drifts back at roughly flight speed U, a follower Δx behind needs a timing offset of about 2π·f·Δx/U, plus π if directly behind; that formula is derived from the paper's definition of spatial phase, not quoted from it.

## A ten-parameter rig turns the biology into boid animation

Earlier animation work shows both the pattern and its gaps. Reynolds' original boids did not flap, and he left the "wing flap cycle" as future work ([Reynolds 1987](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~dt/siggraph97-course/cwr87/)). The three.js GPU birds example advances a per-bird phase faster with horizontal speed and climb rate and moves the two wingtip vertices with a fixed-height sine, with no roll, no change in flap height, no gliding, no difference between upstroke and downstroke and no folding ([three.js source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/examples/webgl_gpgpu_birds.html)). Wu & Popović's physics-based birds supply curve shapes that are cheap to reuse: a cosine for the up-down flapping angle (dihedral), a second curve that holds the wing still through the downstroke and then folds and unfolds it quickly during the upstroke, a phase warp that sets the downstroke's share of the beat, and blending of consecutive wingbeats at mid-upstroke where the wing is neutral ([Wu & Popović 2003](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/flight/wu2003realistic.pdf)). Their full optimization took 218–324 minutes for 3.8–6.7 seconds of flight, so it is offline only, but the curves cost almost nothing per vertex. Epic's *Matrix Awakens* demo instead used baked vertex-animation textures on simple bird meshes flocked with Niagara ([befores & afters](https://beforesandafters.com/2022/04/04/how-to-sim-birds/), summary only), and the same phase value can index such a texture if a richer artist-made cycle is wanted.

The sketch below combines those curves with the biology above. It is my own synthesis, and its constants are starting points to tune, not values from any one source.

```js
// Per boid each frame. phase in [0, 2π), 0 = top of the stroke. tau = downstroke share.
const aFwd = dot(accel, fwd);                     // forward acceleration (braking < 0)
const climb = vel.y / speed;
// Tempo: nearly flat in cruise; up to ~1.35x for hard acceleration (pigeon takeoff), a little for climb.
const hz = baseHz * (1 + 0.35 * clamp(aFwd / A_MAX, 0, 1) + 0.08 * Math.max(climb, 0));
if (mode === 'flap') phase = (phase + 2 * Math.PI * hz * dt) % (2 * Math.PI);

// Warp so the downstroke takes tau of the cycle (house martin 0.32-0.41; pigeon ~0.45).
const p = phase / (2 * Math.PI);
const ph = 2 * Math.PI * (p < tau ? 0.5 * p / tau : 0.5 + 0.5 * (p - tau) / (1 - tau));

// Flap angle (Wu & Popović g1) and an upstroke-only fold pulse (g2) peaking mid-upstroke.
const amp = effort * strokeAngle;                    // ~90° swallow cruise; wider when accelerating
const elevation = midAngle + 0.5 * amp * Math.cos(ph);
const fold = ph < Math.PI ? 0 : 0.5 * (1 - Math.cos(2 * ph));
let span = 1 - fold * (1 - upstrokeSpan);           // upstrokeSpan ~0.3-0.4 for hirundines

if (mode === 'glide') span = clamp(1.25 - speed / (4 * vMin), 0.33, 1); // jackdaw/hawk rule
if (mode === 'bound') span = boundSpan;             // wings tucked (small birds only)
if (mode === 'pause') { /* hold phase near mid-upstroke, wings flexed (swallow) */ }

// Bank toward lateral acceleration: quick roll-in, slower roll-out, no overshoot (high damping).
const aLat = sub(accel, scale(fwd, aFwd));
const bankTarget = clamp(Math.atan2(dot(aLat, right), G), -BANK_MAX, BANK_MAX);
bank += (bankTarget - bank) * (1 - Math.exp(-(Math.abs(bankTarget) > Math.abs(bank) ? ROLL_IN : ROLL_OUT) * dt));

// Pitch the body with acceleration; the stroke plane rides along at a fixed body angle.
const pitch = cruisePitch - (aFwd > 0 ? PITCH_ACCEL : PITCH_BRAKE) * aFwd;
```

The state machine around the sketch follows the intermittent-flight evidence. A boid should switch to **flap** when climbing, accelerating or below cruise speed, and to **glide** above cruise speed when little thrust is needed or when descending; small birds should **bound** in place of gliding at high speed, and swallows should take short mid-upstroke **pauses** at medium to high speed. Mode changes belong near mid-upstroke, following Wu & Popović's blend point, with `effort` smoothed so the wings never pop.

### Where this repo's swallows diverge from the biology

The current code already has a flap-glide switch that matches how swallows fly intermittently, hysteresis so birds don't flicker between states, and a per-boid spread in wingbeat rate. The table lists where `src/boids.js` and `src/swallows.js` part from the evidence, and the matching fix.

| Current behaviour | Biology-based change |
|---|---|
| `wingPhase` advances uniformly. `Math.sin(wingPhase)` gives equal downstroke and upstroke halves, and `fly()` pushes on `-cos(wingPhase)`. | Warp the phase so the downstroke takes about 35–45% of the cycle. Do the warp once in `boids.js` so the thrust pulse and the drawn wing stay in sync. |
| Visible span is `cos(elevation)` only, so upstroke and downstroke look identical at the same elevation. | Multiply by an upstroke-only fold pulse that bottoms out near 0.35 at mid-upstroke, the house martin's span ratio. From below this is the most visible single change. |
| At full effort the stroke always runs from `WING_UP` 75° to `WING_DOWN` 55°, a 130° arc. | Use about 90° in cruise, the Nudds fit for a swallow-sized span. Reserve the wide arc for bursts of hard acceleration and climbing. |
| `effort` eases between 0 and 1 based only on the flap/glide flag. | Drive the target effort from forward acceleration and climb rate, so amplitude rises with demand. |
| Gliding means wings fully spread (elevation 0, span 1) at any speed. | Set glide span from speed using the jackdaw rule, and sweep the hand back when fast. Add short mid-upstroke pauses with wings flexed, as barn swallows and house martins do. |
| `wingHz` is fixed per boid at 3.2 ± 0.6 Hz. | Keep the base rate, but raise it about 35% for hard acceleration, 5–10% when climbing, and slightly with crowding. |
| Sprites rotate only within the screen plane, and boids never bank. | Compute bank from lateral acceleration with quick roll-in and slower roll-out. For a bird seen from below, also scale silhouette width by about cos(bank). This produces the murmuration-style shimmer. |

The absolute wingbeat rate is a judgment call rather than a sourced finding. Real swallows flap at 9–12 Hz, which at 60 frames per second leaves only 5–7 frames per beat: too few to show a fast downstroke and slow upstroke, and close enough to the frame rate to strobe. The repo's slower 3.2 Hz base reads better on screen, and the realism comes from keeping the ratios (takeoff at about 1.35× cruise, landing at about 1.18×, a few percent extra when climbing, and spread between individuals) more than from the absolute number.

## Conclusion

The main correction to the usual boids flap is that, for most birds, steady speed is not something to show through wingbeat tempo. Tying flap rate to speed, as the three.js example does, fits starlings but not pigeons, magpies or swallows in steady flight; the more faithful mapping ties tempo and amplitude to *changes* in motion (accelerating, braking, climbing) and lets steady speed show through body pitch, glide span and how much the upstroke folds. Two simplifications fall out of the evidence and make the rig cheaper than it looks: because the stroke plane is fixed to the body, one pitch value can carry takeoff, cruise and landing, and because roll is what a viewer reads at flock scale, getting banking right in a murmuration matters more than any detail on a single wing.

The gaps in the evidence set the limits of this advice. No measured roll rates, bank limits or upstroke span ratios were found for starlings or barn swallows, downstroke fractions are missing for large birds and for takeoff, the flock-pigeon data mapping flap amplitude to speed (Usherwood et al. 2011) were not accessible, and the direction in which span ratio changes with speed is disputed. Those parameters need tuning by eye against slow-motion footage, with the house martin and cliff swallow data as the closest anchors for a swallow flock.
